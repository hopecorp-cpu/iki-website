Osteoarthritis: Signs, Causes, and When to See a Doctor

On some mornings, the very first step out of bed comes with a stiff knee, and you have to stand for a moment until the joint "loosens up" before you can walk. Some people hear a grating sound in the knee going up and down stairs, or feel a sharp twinge when standing up from a squat. These familiar experiences are often called joint pain in general, and most are linked to osteoarthritis, the most common age-related joint condition. Understanding it correctly helps you know when to worry and what to do to keep your joints more resilient over the years.

This article provides reference knowledge and does not replace examination or diagnosis by a doctor.

What osteoarthritis is

At each joint, the two ends of bone that meet are covered by a smooth layer of cartilage that acts as a cushion, helping the bones glide over each other gently and absorbing shock during movement. Around the joint there is also lubricating joint fluid, along with ligaments and muscles that hold it steady.

Osteoarthritis occurs when this cushioning cartilage gradually wears down, thins out, and loses its smoothness over time. As the cartilage thins, the cushioning space between the two bone ends narrows. In advanced stages, the cartilage can wear down to the point where bone rubs directly against bone, causing pain and limiting movement. The body responds by forming bone spurs at the edges of the joint, making the joint rough and gradually deformed.

This is a process that unfolds slowly over many years, most often in joints that bear heavy loads and are used a lot, such as the knees, hips, lower spine, cervical vertebrae, and the small joints of the hands. Because it progresses slowly, many people only notice it once the pain clearly affects daily life.

Common signs

The symptoms of osteoarthritis build up gradually over time and rarely appear suddenly. Domestic and international medical sources describe the following typical signs fairly consistently:

  • Pain in the joint during movement. The pain increases when walking, climbing stairs, squatting, or carrying loads, and usually eases with rest. This is the earliest and most common sign.
  • Morning stiffness. The joint feels stiff and hard to move when you first wake up or after sitting still for a while. This stiffness usually does not last long, only a few dozen minutes, then eases once you start moving again.
  • A grating sound in the joint. When you bend or straighten it, the joint may make a cracking or grating sound because the surfaces are no longer smooth.
  • Reduced range of motion. The joint is harder to fully bend or straighten as before, with a feeling of catching and less flexibility.
  • Swelling and tenderness. In more advanced stages, the joint may be slightly swollen, warm, and tender to the touch, along with mild weakness and wasting of the muscles around it.

When the condition is severe, some cases involve joint deformity and misalignment, which changes the way a person walks, makes walking difficult, and increases the risk of losing balance. In the hands, the finger joints may develop hard knobs at the knuckles.

Causes and risk factors

Osteoarthritis is divided into two groups by cause. The primary group, also called idiopathic, is tied to the natural aging process: with age, the composition of joint cartilage changes and its ability to repair itself gradually declines, so the cartilage wears down more easily. The secondary group is when there is an identifiable cause that damages the joint earlier than usual, such as an old injury or a joint deformity.

The factors recognized as increasing the risk of osteoarthritis include:

  • Older age. This is the leading risk factor, as cartilage ages gradually over time.
  • Being overweight or obese. Extra weight places a heavy load on weight-bearing joints such as the knees and hips. In addition, fat tissue produces substances that may contribute to inflammation around the joint.
  • Sex. Women, especially after menopause, have a higher rate.
  • Joint injury. Injuries from accidents or sports, even those that happened many years ago, can increase the risk of degeneration later on.
  • Excessive and repetitive joint use. Work that requires kneeling, squatting, carrying heavy loads, or performing a repeated motion on the same joint over a long time.
  • Muscle weakness and joint laxity. Weak muscles around the joint do not support it well, causing the joint to bear force unevenly.
  • Genetic factors. Some people carry genetic traits for less durable joint cartilage, leading to faster joint wear.
  • Bone density and certain other joint conditions are also considered contributing factors.

Recognizing the risk factors helps you understand why you may be prone to joint pain and take active steps to adjust what can be changed, such as weight and movement habits.

When to see a doctor

Many people are used to enduring joint pain for a long time, buying pain relievers to get through the day without seeing a doctor. This can mask the true course of the condition. You should proactively see a doctor when:

  • Joint pain lasts many weeks without easing, or grows worse despite rest.
  • Stiffness and pain make walking, climbing stairs, gripping, or daily activities difficult.
  • The joint is clearly swollen, warm, and red, or shows signs of deformity and misalignment.
  • The pain disrupts your sleep, affecting your mood and quality of life.

Some signs should be treated as warning signs, and you should see a doctor early so they can rule out causes other than osteoarthritis:

  • Sudden, marked joint swelling with severe pain and fever. This may indicate a joint infection or another form of arthritis needing timely medical attention.
  • Prolonged morning stiffness lasting a very long time, pain in many joints at once and symmetrically on both sides, which may suggest an inflammatory joint condition different from osteoarthritis.
  • Joint pain accompanied by unexplained weight loss and general fatigue.

During the examination, the doctor will take your history, examine the joint, and may order an X-ray or tests to determine the extent and rule out other causes. Choosing the direction of care and intervention is a professional decision made by the doctor, according to each person's stage and individual condition.

Lifestyle care and prevention

Osteoarthritis is tied to age and cannot be fully prevented, but many lifestyle habits can help reduce the burden on the joints, limit pain, and keep the joints moving well for longer. The suggestions below are general ways of caring for yourself, not a course of management that replaces a doctor's instructions.

Keep a healthy weight. This is one of the things with the greatest impact on the knee and hip joints. A moderate weight clearly reduces the pressure on weight-bearing joints, which in turn eases the sensation of pain. Weight should be managed through a balanced diet and regular movement.

Move appropriately, regularly, and gently. Complete rest is not good for joints, because the muscles around them will weaken. Forms of low-impact movement such as walking, swimming, cycling, tai chi, or stretching exercises are encouraged by many experts. Movement helps the muscles around the joint grow stronger, support the joint better, and maintain flexibility. Start slowly and increase gradually, avoiding movements that cause sharp pain.

Protect your joints in daily life. Pay attention to correct posture when sitting, standing, and carrying loads. Limit prolonged squatting or kneeling, and avoid lifting heavy objects incorrectly. When lifting, use the strength of your legs rather than bending your back abruptly.

Take care of your meals. A balanced diet rich in leafy greens and fruit, with enough protein and nutrients for the bones such as calcium and vitamin D, contributes to the overall health of the musculoskeletal system. Eating in moderation also helps keep your weight stable.

Rest sensibly and listen to your body. Alternate movement with rest, and avoid pushing one joint to work too hard over a long period. Warm or cool compresses may bring temporary comfort for some people, but if the pain persists you should still see a doctor.

Frequently asked questions

Does only the elderly get osteoarthritis? Older age is the leading risk factor, but it is not only older people who are affected. Younger people who have had a joint injury, are overweight, or work in jobs that put repetitive pressure on a joint can also develop osteoarthritis early. So joint care should begin while you are still healthy.

Is a grating sound in the joint during movement something to worry about? A sound in the joint is not always a sign of disease; many people have joint sounds without any pain. What deserves attention is when the grating comes with pain, swelling, stiffness, or reduced movement. In that case you should see a doctor for an assessment.

If I have joint pain, should I rest or move? Complete rest over a long period usually leaves the muscles weak and the joint stiffer. On the other hand, appropriate gentle movement is beneficial for the joints. The principle is to choose low-impact exercises, work within your limits, and avoid movements that increase pain. If you are unsure what level suits you, ask a doctor or a physical therapist.

How does keeping a healthy weight help the joints? For weight-bearing joints such as the knees and hips, every bit of extra weight adds pressure on the joint when walking. Keeping your weight at a reasonable level helps reduce this load, and as a result many people find they have less pain and move more easily. This is one of the most widely encouraged lifestyle changes.

Does changing weather make joints hurt more? Some people feel their joints ache more when it turns cold or damp. This sensation is real for many people, though the mechanism is not entirely clear. Keeping the joints warm, moving gently, and dressing warmly in cold weather may help you feel more comfortable.

In closing

Osteoarthritis is part of the natural aging process, but how much it affects your life depends greatly on how you care for your joints each day. Prolonged joint pain is not something to quietly endure, nor something to handle carelessly on your own. Listening to early signals such as morning stiffness or pain during movement, combined with keeping a healthy weight, moving regularly, and seeing a doctor at the right time, is how you keep your joints serving you more durably.

This article provides reference knowledge and does not replace examination or diagnosis by a doctor. When joint pain persists or you notice anything unusual, please visit a medical facility for advice from a specialist doctor.
