Starting Solids: Where to Begin So Baby Eats Well and Mom Worries Less

IKI Health Coach Team· Updated 2026-08-11· 9 min read
Quick answer

Solids are usually best introduced around 6 months of age, once your baby can sit steadily, has good head and neck control, and shows curiosity about food. Start with soft, smooth, lightly seasoned foods, and gradually increase texture as your baby grows so they can naturally learn to chew and swallow.

Starting solids is a milestone that most Vietnamese parents feel both excited and nervous about. Excited, because your baby is about to explore a world of food beyond breast milk or formula—but nervous, too, because there's so much conflicting advice out there: should you start with rice cereal or mashed vegetables, follow a traditional spoon-feeding approach or baby-led weaning, add seasoning or not. This article won't hand you a rigid formula. Instead, it offers a flexible path based on your baby's natural development and what fits well with Vietnamese family meals—so you can worry less while your baby still eats well.

Why the timing of starting solids matters

A newborn's digestive system needs time to develop the enzymes needed to break down starches, proteins, and complex fats. If solids are introduced too early, before the digestive system is ready, babies can experience bloating, indigestion, or even reduced milk intake—which is still the main source of nutrition in early life. On the other hand, starting too late may mean missing the ideal window for getting used to new tastes and textures, which can lead to picky eating or slower development of chewing and swallowing skills later on.

The World Health Organization recommends around 6 months as a suitable age to begin solids for most babies, but this is a general guideline rather than a strict rule for every child. Some babies are ready a little earlier, while others need a few more weeks to develop the ability to sit up and control their head and neck. What matters most is that parents watch for signs of readiness rather than relying solely on the number of months on the calendar.

Signs that your baby is ready for solids

One common mistake is starting solids too soon simply because other babies the same age have already begun, even though your own baby isn't quite ready in terms of motor skills and digestion. To recognize the right timing, there are a few clear signs you can observe at home.

  • Your baby can sit steadily with support and hold their head up stably, without wobbling.
  • Your baby shows curiosity about adult food, following a spoon with their eyes or reaching for food on the table.
  • The tongue-thrust reflex—which helps babies nurse—has lessened, so food isn't automatically pushed back out of the mouth.
  • Your baby can intentionally bring their hands or objects to their mouth, showing developing hand-to-mouth coordination.

If your baby shows all these signs, it's a reasonable time to start solids, whether or not they've hit exactly 6 months. If not, there's no need to rush—you can simply wait a little longer.

Choosing foods for the early stage (around 6–8 months)

The early stage of solids isn't about replacing milk feedings with full meals—it's mainly about helping your baby get used to new tastes, textures, and the motion of chewing and swallowing. Breast milk or formula remains the main source of nutrition, while solids play a supporting, practice role.

In the first weeks, choose soft, smooth, easy-to-digest foods with low allergy risk, such as steamed and mashed pumpkin, mashed sweet potato, mashed ripe banana, steamed and mashed carrot, or thin rice porridge. Each new food should be introduced on its own for about three to five days before adding another, so you can easily watch for any unusual reactions.

As for seasoning, this stage calls for minimal salt, sugar, and strong spices. A baby's kidneys aren't yet able to process sodium the way an adult's can, so early meals are best kept close to the food's natural flavor. This also helps your baby experience and remember the true taste of vegetables, meat, and fish from an early age—a good foundation for healthy eating habits later on.

8–10 months: expanding textures and flavors

Once your baby is comfortable with smooth purées, it's time to gradually increase texture to encourage chewing. Instead of fully puréed food, you can move to a slightly lumpy mash or soft, finely chopped pieces, letting your baby use their gums and any emerging teeth to work on the food.

This is also a good time to introduce more protein-rich foods such as chicken, lean pork, boneless freshwater fish, tofu, and well-cooked egg yolk. Protein plays a key role in muscle and brain development, so it's best not to rely on starches and vegetables alone for too long. Families interested in ensuring a well-rounded protein intake may find it helpful to read signs your body may be low on protein to understand why this group of nutrients deserves attention right from the start of solids.

At this stage, babies usually eat solids two to three times a day, alongside milk feedings. Pay attention to your baby's hunger and fullness cues—if they turn away, close their mouth, or push the spoon away, that's a sign they've had enough, and there's no need to encourage more.

10–12 months and beyond: introducing more texture and family meals

From around 10 months, many babies can pick up food with their hands and bring it to their mouth on their own. This is a good time to introduce soft foods cut into sticks or bite-sized pieces, such as steamed pumpkin sticks, sliced banana, or soft bread torn into small pieces. This approach, sometimes called baby-led weaning, helps develop hand-eye coordination and lets your baby regulate their own intake.

Around the one-year mark is also when babies can start sitting with the family during meals, even though their food still needs to be prepared separately—softer and with less seasoning than adult meals. Sitting at the table together not only lets your baby observe and learn from how adults eat, but also helps build eating habits within a warm, connected atmosphere—something many Vietnamese families still value in every shared meal. This rhythm echoes the spirit described in the article on multi-generational family meals, where eating together is about connection as much as nourishment.

Building positive eating habits from the very first meals

One thing parents often overlook is that the atmosphere at mealtime matters just as much as how much food a baby eats. If every meal comes with pressure, scolding, or threats, a baby can easily develop fear or avoidance around eating later on. On the other hand, a relaxed, low-pressure mealtime helps your baby see eating as a pleasant experience, encouraging them to explore new foods on their own instead of being forced to swallow.

Young children learn how to eat not only from what goes into their mouth, but also from the emotions and atmosphere surrounding each meal.

To nurture positive habits, parents can apply a few simple principles: keep mealtimes consistent, avoid screens like TV or phones during meals, let your baby hold their own spoon or feed themselves once they're ready, and avoid forcing them to finish a portion once they show signs of fullness. Repeating familiar meals alongside new ones also helps your baby feel secure while still gradually broadening their palate.

Common mistakes when starting solids

Many parents, worried about their baby not getting enough nutrients, unintentionally make mistakes that add unnecessary stress to mealtimes. Some common ones include the following.

  • Over-seasoning food with salt or sugar to suit adult tastes, which gets babies used to strong flavors too early.
  • Forcing a baby to finish a prepared portion even after showing signs of fullness, creating long-term psychological pressure.
  • Starting solids too early or too late relative to the baby's actual signs of readiness.
  • Focusing only on starches and avoiding vegetables with distinct flavors, like certain leafy greens, which means missing an early chance to build a taste for variety.

Changing the menu too quickly or introducing too many new foods at once also makes it harder to track how your baby is reacting. Patiently introducing one food at a time and watching closely for a few days remains the safest and most effective approach.

A sample eating rhythm for the first week

For families just starting out, a gentle rhythm for the first week might look like this: on the first few days, introduce just one type of smoothly mashed vegetable, one to two small spoonfuls at a time, at one feeding per day. Once your baby is comfortable and shows no unusual reactions, you can add a second food, then gradually combine two to three foods in one meal as your baby becomes more skilled at eating.

There's no need to rush into an elaborate menu in the very first week. The main goal at this stage is for your baby to get used to the spoon, new flavors, and the sensation of swallowing thicker food than milk—not to meet their full daily nutritional needs just yet. For babies who may need extra attention to nutrients important for height development, parents can also read more about a diet that supports height growth in children for a broader view as your baby grows.

What to do when your baby refuses food or resists something new

It's completely normal for a baby to turn their head away, press their lips shut, or spit food out the first few times they try something new—it doesn't mean they'll dislike it forever. Research on children's eating behavior suggests it may take eight to ten exposures to a new food before a baby truly accepts it. So instead of giving up after the first refusal, try offering the food again another time, change how it's prepared, or pair it with something your baby already enjoys.

If picky eating persists and starts affecting your baby's weight or mood, it's worth talking with a pediatrician for guidance suited to your child's specific needs. You may also find practical tips in the article what to do when a child is a picky eater for gentle, low-pressure approaches for both you and your baby.

Starting solids and the journey toward lifelong healthy eating habits

Starting solids isn't just a brief transition in the first few months of life—it lays the groundwork for your baby's eating habits for years to come. A child who is introduced to vegetables early, in a relaxed atmosphere free of pressure, tends to grow up eating a wider variety of foods and being less picky. On the other hand, if this stage revolves around just a few familiar foods or is tied to stress, a child may carry a reluctance to try new foods well into their school years.

As your child grows into school age, keeping up a full, balanced breakfast is a natural continuation of the journey that began with starting solids. You can read more in the article on breakfast for school-age children to help prepare the right nutritional foundation for each stage of your child's growth.

Every baby develops at their own pace, and there's no single formula for starting solids that works perfectly for every child. The best thing parents can do is observe their baby, stay patient through every attempt at a new food, and keep mealtimes a gentle experience rather than a stressful one. That, more than anything, is the lasting gift you can give your child, starting from the very first spoonful.

If you're getting ready to start your baby on solids, try spending this week observing their signs of readiness, pick one familiar vegetable to introduce first, and give both yourself and your baby time to ease into it, without rushing.

Frequently asked questions

Can my baby start solids before any teeth come in?

Yes. Babies can use their gums to mash soft food, so there's no need to wait for teeth. What matters most is that the food's texture matches your baby's current chewing and swallowing ability.

Should I go fully baby-led from the start?

You can flexibly combine spoon-feeding with letting your baby self-feed, depending on each baby's motor readiness. Many families find that combining both approaches helps ensure adequate intake while also building independence.

Does my baby still need milk once they start solids?

During the first year, breast milk or formula remains the main source of nutrition, with solids playing a supporting role. The amount of milk can gradually decrease as your baby grows and becomes more skilled at eating solids.
