Trouble Sleeping and Tossing at Night: An Evening Routine to Fall Asleep More Easily

IKI Health Coach Team· Updated 2026-07-22· 15 min read
Quick answer

When you have trouble sleeping and often toss and turn, building a steady evening routine usually helps your body fall asleep far more easily than forcing yourself to sleep. Keep a fixed bedtime and wake time so your body clock stays steady, cut blue light from screens about an hour before bed, eat a moderate dinner, and avoid coffee in the late afternoon and evening. A familiar wind-down ritual — dimming the lights, soaking your feet in warm water, breathing slowly, or a cup of warm herbal tea — signals to your body that it's time to rest. Getting early morning sunlight and gentle daytime movement also make the night more comfortable.

There's a feeling many people know all too well: you're in bed, the lights are off, but your mind keeps running. The things left undone, the words spoken during the day, tomorrow's plans all come flooding back. You turn over, glance at the clock, see how late it is, and grow even more anxious. The harder you try to sleep, the more awake you feel. By morning you're weary, your head is heavy, and a new day begins already short on energy.

If this sounds familiar, here's some good news: very often the key isn't forcing yourself to sleep, but what you do in the one to two hours before you get into bed. A steady, gentle evening routine can make a big difference to falling asleep. This article walks you through looking back at your own evenings and building a more comfortable routine, as part of caring for your health proactively each day.

Why we toss and turn

Sleep isn't something we switch on and off at will like a light. It comes when both body and mind feel safe, relaxed, and ready to rest. The trouble is that modern life often has us getting into bed while our whole self is still in "running" mode.

Picture a typical evening: a late, heavy dinner, then sitting down to finish up work on the computer, then lying in bed scrolling on the phone until sleepiness comes. Blue light from the screen, a constant stream of information, and unsorted thoughts keep the mind in a stimulated state. By the time you decide to sleep, your body still hasn't received a clear signal that it's time to rest.

Another common cause is an irregular schedule. Tonight you sleep at ten, tomorrow at one in the morning, on the weekend you stay up late and sleep in. The body's clock likes regularity; when your bedtime keeps shifting, the body struggles to know when to feel sleepy and when to be alert, and the result is restlessness.

Worry is another familiar culprit. A busy day leaves us no time to process emotions and thoughts; only when we lie still in the dark does everything come rushing in. This is why many people find their minds are "noisiest" precisely when they get into bed. Understanding these causes makes one thing clear: to sleep more easily, care for the stretch of time before bed rather than waiting until you lie down to begin.

An evening routine begins in the afternoon

What surprises many people is that a good night's sleep is often prepared for early, even from the afternoon, not just in the few minutes before bed.

Coffee and strong tea are the clearest examples. Caffeine can linger in the body for hours, so a late-afternoon cup of coffee sometimes still affects your night's sleep. Many people choose to stop coffee and strong tea from early afternoon, and switch to plain water or a caffeine-free drink for the rest of the day.

Daytime movement also plays an important part. A little gentle activity such as walking, doing housework, or stretching between tasks helps the body spend energy and feel naturally tired by evening. That said, avoid vigorous exercise close to bedtime, as it can leave the body keyed up and harder to settle. You can read more in Morning Habits of Healthy Living to place movement in a sensible daily rhythm.

Daylight is a natural signal for the body clock. Getting a little morning sun helps the body clearly distinguish day from night, so it's easier to feel sleepy on time in the evening. Conversely, if you spend all day in a dimly lit room and then get lots of artificial light at night, the body can easily confuse its day-night rhythm.

Dinner is another link in the chain. Eating too much or too late leaves your stomach heavy and uncomfortable when you lie down; eating too little can leave you hungry in the middle of the night. Many people find it comfortable to eat dinner about three hours before bed, in a moderate portion with little deep-fried food. If you're a little hungry before bed, an easy-to-digest snack such as yogurt or a banana is usually better than a large meal.

Building a wind-down ritual before bed

If the afternoon is the foundation, the hour or so before bed is when you send your body its clearest signal: it's time to rest. A familiar wind-down ritual, repeated each evening, gradually becomes a "soft switch" that helps you shift from day mode to night mode.

This ritual needn't be elaborate. What matters is that it's gentle, comfortable, and repeated steadily. Below are a few options you can combine into a routine of your own.

Dimming the lights is a good first step. About an hour before bed, turn off the bright overhead lights, use a soft warm lamp, and most importantly, step away from phone and computer screens. If you must use them, switch on warm-light mode and lower the brightness. Soft light helps the body understand that night has fallen.

A quiet, familiar activity helps the mind settle: reading a few pages of a paper book, listening to soft music, writing a few lines in a journal, or simply sitting still. For those who tend to worry, writing down unfinished tasks before bed is a way to "set them aside" out of your head, so the mind has less to hold onto.

Warmth is a natural relaxation signal. A warm bath, or soaking your feet in warm water for about ten to fifteen minutes, brings a pleasant feeling and helps the body ease into a more restful state. It's a simple, inexpensive ritual that many people love.

Slow, mindful breathing is another useful tool. You might try breathing in slowly for a count of four, holding gently, then breathing out a little longer, repeating a few times. Slow breathing helps the body settle and quiets a racing mind.

For many people, a cup of warm, caffeine-free herbal tea is also a pleasant part of the evening ritual, thanks to the warmth and the moment of slowing down as you sip it unhurriedly. You can see more on choosing and brewing it in Warm daily drinks and herbal tea to fold it gently into your own evening routine. This is a habit that helps create a relaxed feeling before bed, not a substitute for comprehensive sleep care.

If you like gentle technology reminders to keep a steady routine, setting a bedtime reminder or logging your evening routine in the IKI App can help you keep the habit each day without having to remember it manually.

Setting up your bedroom for comfort

Your sleep space matters more than you might think. A well-arranged bedroom helps the body drift off more easily.

Keep light as low as possible. Thick curtains, turning off small blinking lights, and covering light from electronics all help make the room calmer. Darkness is a familiar signal for the body to prepare for rest.

A moderately cool temperature is usually more comfortable for sleep than too hot or too cold. Many people find a slightly cool room with a warm blanket to be the ideal combination. Adjust by season and your own preference.

Quiet helps sleep stay unbroken. If your surroundings are noisy, you might try earplugs, or a soft, steady background sound to mask sudden noises. Bed and pillows should also be tidy and clean, so it feels pleasant to lie down.

One principle worth remembering: keep the bed for sleep and rest, and limit working or scrolling on the phone in bed. When the bed is linked with rest in your mind, lying down will naturally invite a sleepier feeling.

An evening routine by constitution and circumstance

There's no single evening routine that's right for everyone. Observing yourself in order to adjust is exactly the spirit of proactive wellness.

People who tend to worry, whose minds are hard to quiet, should prioritize activities that help "tidy the mind" before bed, such as journaling, listing tomorrow's tasks, or slow breathing. For this group, stepping away from screens early is especially important, since a constant flow of information makes the mind even harder to settle.

People who often feel cold, with cold hands and feet in the evening, usually feel more comfortable with warmth: a warm foot soak, airy but warm-enough sleepwear, and a suitable blanket. A cup of a mild warm drink before bed also suits this group.

People who work shifts or keep irregular hours will find it hard to fix an absolute bedtime, but can still keep a familiar wind-down ritual each time they prepare to sleep, even when the hours change. That repeated ritual is the stable signal the body holds onto.

Older adults naturally tend to have lighter sleep and wake more easily. This doesn't mean they need less sleep; rather, the body needs its habits tended to more carefully: keeping a regular schedule, getting early sunlight, gentle daytime movement, and a relaxing evening. If sleep declines noticeably and causes lasting fatigue, it's best to consult a doctor.

Adjusting by constitution isn't a rigid rule, but a way of listening and fine-tuning over time. If your sleep trouble is severe, lasts for many weeks, and clearly affects your daily life, talk to a doctor or a sleep specialist for suitable support.

Common misconceptions about sleep

Around sleep trouble there are quite a few beliefs that only make people more anxious. Let's untangle a few common knots.

"Just lie in bed long enough and you'll fall asleep somehow." In reality, forcing yourself to stay in bed while tense, constantly checking the clock, usually leaves you more awake and links the bed with restlessness. If you're still restless after about twenty minutes, getting out of bed to do something very gentle and returning when you feel sleepy is usually better than lying there forcing it.

"A little alcohol helps you sleep." Alcohol may make you feel drowsy at first, but it usually makes later sleep more fitful and less deep. It isn't a sustainable way to support sleep.

"Catching up on the weekend is enough." Sleeping a lot on the weekend may relieve tiredness temporarily, but big shifts in your schedule throw the body clock further off, making it hard to fall asleep at the start of the week. Everyday consistency is more sustainable than binge-sleeping.

"Watching your phone until you're sleepy is the only way to fall asleep." The light and stream of information from screens usually keep the mind more alert, dragging out the tossing and turning. Stepping away from screens early and replacing them with a quiet activity usually makes it easier to drift off.

Understanding sleep correctly helps you worry less and decide with more confidence, instead of chasing word-of-mouth tips that sometimes backfire.

In closing

Trouble sleeping and tossing at night is something a great many people face, and in most cases the key isn't forcing yourself to sleep but caring for the time before bed. A steady, gentle evening routine, repeated each day, gradually helps the body understand the "time to rest" signal and drift off more easily.

Start with one small change tonight. It might be putting your phone away from the bed, dimming the lights a little earlier, soaking your feet in warm water, or writing a few lines to lighten the mind. These small, steady steps, together with a regular schedule and a sun-lit morning, are how you care for your sleep and your health. If you'd like to go deeper into building habits for a good night and a clear day, see also Quality Sleep: Habits for Good Nights and Clear Days. Comfortable sleep doesn't come from one perfect night, but from a "good enough" evening routine kept up steadily.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I keep tossing and turning even when I go to bed early?

Going to bed early while your mind is still racing after work, your phone, or worries keeps your body in an alert state, so it's hard to drift off. Many people find it easier to fall asleep when they create a relaxing buffer before bed: stepping away from screens, dimming the lights, and doing a few gentle, familiar things to let the mind settle. If the tossing and turning goes on for a long time and noticeably affects your days, it's worth talking to a doctor.

What does an evening routine that helps sleep include?

A comfortable evening routine often includes: a moderate dinner that isn't too late, stopping coffee from early afternoon, cutting blue light about an hour before bed, keeping the bedroom dark, cool, and quiet, and a familiar wind-down ritual such as a warm bath, a foot soak, reading a few pages, or slow breathing. What matters most is repeating it steadily so your body gets used to the rhythm.

Does eating dinner late make it harder to sleep?

Eating too much or too late can leave your stomach heavy and uncomfortable when you lie down, making sleep more fitful for some people. Many find it more comfortable to eat dinner about three hours before bed, in a moderate portion with little deep-fried food. If you're a little hungry before bed, an easy-to-digest snack like yogurt or a banana is usually gentler than a large meal.

How does blue light from my phone in the evening affect me?

Blue light from screens in the evening can make it harder for the body to recognize the signal that night has fallen and it's time to rest, so you stay alert longer. Cutting screen use about an hour before bed, switching to a warm-light mode, lowering brightness, and keeping the phone away from the bed are simple habits worth trying to help the evening settle.

Does herbal tea in the evening help me sleep more easily?

Many people treat a cup of warm, mild, caffeine-free herbal tea in the evening as part of a wind-down ritual that helps the body slow down before rest, thanks to the warmth and the quiet moment it creates. It's a habit that supports a more comfortable lead-up to sleep, not a substitute for comprehensive sleep care. Individual experiences vary.

What should I do if I wake up in the middle of the night and can't fall back asleep?

Waking once or twice during the night is fairly normal. The key is not to get tense or keep checking the clock, since worry only makes you more alert. Keep the room dark, breathe slowly, and let your body settle on its own. If you're still restless after about 20 minutes, you can get out of bed, do something very gentle under soft light, and return when you feel sleepy. If it goes on for a long time, talk to a doctor.

How long before an evening routine helps me sleep more easily?

This varies from person to person and depends on how consistent you are. The body needs time to get used to a new rhythm, so instead of expecting change in a night or two, keep the routine for a few weeks and observe how you feel. If your sleep trouble is severe, persistent, and clearly affects your daytime health, you should see a doctor or a sleep specialist.
