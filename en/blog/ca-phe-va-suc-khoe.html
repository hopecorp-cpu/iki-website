Coffee and Health: How to Enjoy It Well and Avoid Sleeplessness and Jitters

IKI Health Coach Team· Updated 2026-07-24· 13 min read
Quick answer

For most healthy adults, drinking coffee the right way means: keeping to a moderate amount (about 1–3 cups a day, under 400mg of caffeine), drinking it mid-morning after a light bite rather than on an empty stomach, and avoiding it late in the afternoon so it doesn't affect your sleep. Observe how you personally feel — if you notice restlessness, a racing heart, an unsettled stomach, or trouble sleeping, cut back. People with heart or blood pressure concerns or a sensitive stomach should ask a doctor about a suitable amount of caffeine.

Coffee is a familiar part of daily life for many Vietnamese people. A morning that begins with a phin filter coffee, a mid-session cup at a café, or an iced milk coffee in the late afternoon — for many, it's not just a drink but a moment to pause in a busy day. But precisely because it's so familiar, we rarely notice whether the way we drink coffee truly suits our body. Some drink it on an empty stomach and feel unsettled; others have a cup late in the afternoon and toss and turn all night. This article shares how to build a proactive coffee habit, so you can enjoy the pleasant lift coffee brings while staying gentle on your stomach and your sleep.

Coffee: benefits and drawbacks, viewed in balance

Coffee is neither a "villain" nor a "miracle cure." The most balanced view is to see it as a drink with its own character: used the right way, it's pleasant; used to excess or at the wrong time, it can be uncomfortable for some people.

What makes coffee so loved is the feeling of alertness and focus it brings — coming from caffeine, a natural stimulant found in coffee beans. Coffee also contains antioxidant compounds and a distinctive flavor that's hard to replace. Many people treat their morning coffee as a small ritual that helps the mind get into gear.

The other side is that caffeine doesn't affect everyone the same way. Caffeine-sensitive people may feel a racing heart, restlessness, shaky hands, or trouble sleeping from even a small amount. Drinking too much, drinking on an empty stomach, or drinking too late in the day are three situations that easily turn the coffee experience from pleasant into tiring.

Coffee is a drink to enjoy in moderation — its value lies in being measured and well-timed, not in drinking as much as possible to "power through" work.

How caffeine affects the body

Understanding a little about caffeine helps you choose how to drink it wisely. This is a general description, not a diagnosis or medical advice for anyone in particular.

When it enters the body, caffeine temporarily reduces the feeling of drowsiness and helps us feel more alert. This effect is usually clearest after about 30–60 minutes. What many people overlook is that caffeine lingers in the body for quite a while: on average, it takes several hours for the body to metabolize half the caffeine you've taken in, and this figure varies with constitution, age, and other factors. That's why a late-afternoon cup can still be quietly at work when you've already gone to bed.

An important point: caffeine doesn't create new energy. It mainly helps us temporarily feel less tired. So coffee can't replace getting enough sleep, eating a balanced diet, and resting properly. When you use coffee to "fight through" a severely sleep-deprived body, the alertness is usually only fleeting, and afterward you tend to feel even more depleted.

Quick reminder

Caffeine helps you feel temporarily more alert; it doesn't "recharge" your energy. Enough sleep, a balanced breakfast, and staying hydrated are the sustainable foundations for natural alertness through the day.

The right time to drink coffee during the day

Timing has a big influence on whether coffee feels pleasant or uncomfortable. A few suggestions for reference:

  • Don't drink it the moment you wake up on an empty stomach. Many people are used to making a cup of coffee the first thing they do, but drinking it before anything is in the stomach easily causes an unsettled feeling. Have a light bite first, then enjoy your coffee a while later.
  • Mid-morning is usually a comfortable time. After you've had breakfast and the body is in rhythm, a mid-morning cup helps you feel more focused in a gentle way.
  • Be careful with the early-afternoon cup. If you need a little alertness after lunch, drink it early in the afternoon rather than leaving it late.
  • Avoid coffee late in the afternoon and in the evening. This is the most important principle for protecting your sleep, and we'll cover it in more detail below.

If you're building a healthy morning, place your coffee after breakfast and a few minutes of light movement. You can also read 7 Morning Habits of Healthy Living to bring your whole morning into rhythm.

A moderate amount: how much is enough

The common reference for a healthy adult is a caffeine intake of under 400mg a day, equivalent to roughly 1–3 cups of coffee depending on strength. This is only a reference figure, not a hard rule, and everyone is sensitive to caffeine differently.

To picture it (these numbers are estimates that vary with how it's brewed):

  • A strong cup of phin filter coffee: usually contains a significant amount of caffeine, so one cup is enough for many people.
  • Instant coffee: usually has less caffeine than machine-brewed or strong phin coffee.
  • Don't forget other sources of caffeine: strong tea, energy drinks, some sodas, and chocolate also add to your daily caffeine.

The best way to self-adjust isn't counting milligrams but listening to your body. If after drinking you notice a racing heart, restlessness, slightly shaky hands, or trouble sleeping at night, that's a signal to cut back or drink earlier. Conversely, if one cup each morning leaves you comfortable and your sleep is still good, that's the amount that suits you.

A small note about sugar: iced milk coffee, ready-made coffee, and "special" coffees at shops usually carry a significant amount of sugar and cream. If you drink them often, that sugar is worth watching even more than the caffeine. Favoring coffee with little or no sugar is gentler for a daily habit.

Why not to drink it on an empty stomach

Coffee has a natural acidity and can stimulate the stomach to produce digestive juices. When the stomach is empty, this easily leads to a queasy, unsettled feeling, even slightly shaky hands or mild dizziness in sensitive people. That's why many who drink coffee before eating anything feel uncomfortable inside, even if their mind is more alert.

The solution is very simple: eat a little something before or along with your coffee. A light breakfast, a few pieces of bread, some fruit, or a plant milk is enough to give the stomach a "cushion" before it takes in coffee. For people with a naturally sensitive stomach, this habit is all the more worth keeping.

In the IKI user community, "drinking coffee after breakfast instead of right when you wake up" is a small adjustment many people share. Many say that simply moving their coffee to after breakfast noticeably reduced that morning unsettled feeling, and they still kept their beloved cup of coffee without having to give it up.

Caffeine and sleep: avoid drinking it late in the afternoon

This is the part that matters most for anyone who tends to toss and turn. Because caffeine takes many hours for the body to metabolize, a cup of coffee drunk in the late afternoon can still be at work when you're getting ready for bed, making sleep shallower and harder to fall into — even when you feel drowsy.

A few suggestions so coffee doesn't disturb your sleep:

  • Set a "curfew" for coffee. Many people find it sensible to stop coffee at least 6–8 hours before bedtime. If you sleep at 10–11pm, your last cup should fall around early afternoon.
  • If you're caffeine-sensitive, push it earlier still. Some people only need coffee after 2–3pm to have trouble sleeping at night. Observing yourself will tell you your threshold.
  • Watch out for hidden caffeine sources in the evening: strong tea, energy drinks, and dark chocolate eaten late can also have an effect.
  • Swap the evening coffee for a caffeine-free warm drink. A cup of warm water or a mild herbal tea is usually a more comfortable choice at the end of the day.

An Eastern medicine view: coffee and constitution

In the treasury of Eastern Traditional Medicine experience, the body is viewed through a balance between tendencies such as cold and heat. This isn't a way to diagnose illness, but a way of observing that helps us care for each person appropriately.

Coffee is often seen as a stimulating drink, one that can make people who already tend to feel "overheated inside," restless, or sleepless feel that state more strongly if they drink a lot. Conversely, some people find coffee warming and, drunk in moderation, well suited to their rhythm of life. This shows that the same drink can feel very different depending on constitution.

The gentlest approach is to observe your own reactions. If you belong to the group that easily feels restless, has a racing heart, or is prone to anxiety and poor sleep, then reducing your coffee dose or choosing a gentler drink usually brings a sense of balance. If you drink in moderation and still feel well and sleep soundly, keep the rhythm that suits you. How to keep coffee gentle: a few small tips

You don't need to give up coffee — just a few small adjustments make the experience much more comfortable:

  • Eat a light bite before drinking so the stomach has a cushion and avoids that unsettled feeling.
  • Drink slowly, savoring each sip rather than downing it in one go — this suits the flavor of coffee and is gentler on the body.
  • Cut back on sugar and cream to avoid taking in extra energy and the heavy feeling afterward.
  • Have a glass of plain water alongside. Coffee can make you need the bathroom more, so topping up water helps the body feel comfortable.
  • Set your last cup for early afternoon to protect your sleep.
  • Listen to your body and adjust the dose by how you feel, rather than drinking out of mechanical habit.

Healthier alternative drinks

If you'd like to cut back on coffee or need another option for the sessions when caffeine isn't suitable, there are plenty of pleasant drinks to alternate with:

  • Green tea: it still has caffeine but usually at a milder level than coffee, with a refreshing flavor. You can read Green Tea and Matcha: the Japanese Daily Tea Habit.
  • Warm herbal tea: most are caffeine-free, suited to the late afternoon and evening. See also Warm Drinks and Herbal Teas: a Daily Warm-Drink Habit.
  • Warm ginger water or warm water with a few lime slices: a gentle option to change things up when you want less coffee.
  • A glass of plain water and a few minutes of light movement: sometimes that mid-session sluggishness isn't from a lack of caffeine but from dehydration or sitting too long. Walking a few minutes and breathing deeply can help you feel alert again naturally.

If you often fall into a mid-session slump and turn to coffee to "fight" it, read Feeling Tired and Sluggish Mid-Session: Causes and What to Eat to understand the root of this feeling.

Who should be cautious with coffee

Coffee doesn't suit everyone at every time. Some groups should be cautious and consider reducing the dose or choosing a lower-caffeine drink:

  • People with heart or blood pressure concerns: caffeine can affect heart rate and blood pressure, so ask a doctor about a suitable amount.
  • People with a sensitive stomach: prone to an unsettled, uncomfortable feeling when drinking coffee, especially on an empty stomach.
  • People prone to anxiety and restlessness: caffeine can make this feeling more pronounced.
  • People who sleep poorly: should pay special attention to the afternoon cup.
  • Pregnant and breastfeeding women: usually advised to limit caffeine; please consult a health professional.
If you have concerns about your heart, blood pressure, or stomach, are pregnant, breastfeeding, or taking medication, please consult a doctor about a suitable amount of caffeine before keeping up a daily coffee habit. This article shares general knowledge only and does not replace medical advice.

A suggested 7-day practice

You don't need to change everything at once. Try a small rhythm over one week to find the way of drinking that suits you:

  • Days 1–2: move your morning coffee to after a light bite, instead of drinking it right when you wake up.
  • Days 3–4: set a "curfew" for coffee in the early afternoon and stick to it.
  • Day 5: try reducing or skipping the sugar in one cup of coffee, and notice how your taste and how you feel inside change.
  • Day 6: swap one cup of coffee for green tea or a warm herbal tea, and see whether you feel comfortable.
  • Day 7: look back over the week — has your sleep, your morning unsettled feeling, and your alertness during the day changed at all — then keep the adjustments that suit you.

Small but consistent changes tend to last far longer than "quitting coffee" abruptly and giving up halfway. The goal isn't to eliminate coffee, but to turn it into a refined habit that suits your body.

In closing: drink coffee as a refined habit

Drinking coffee the right way isn't a complicated secret. It's the combination of a few simple things: keep to a moderate amount, drink it after a light bite, avoid coffee late in the afternoon, and listen to your body to adjust to your own constitution. When you do this, you keep the pleasant moment beside your cup of coffee without paying for it with restless nights or an unsettled stomach.

Frequently asked questions

How much coffee a day is a moderate amount?

For a healthy adult, a common reference is about 1–3 cups of coffee a day, equivalent to under 400mg of caffeine. Everyone is sensitive to caffeine differently, so observe how you personally feel: if you notice a racing heart, restlessness, or trouble sleeping, cut back. Pregnant women and people with heart or blood pressure concerns should consult a doctor about a suitable amount of caffeine.

What time of day is best to drink coffee?

Many people feel comfortable drinking coffee around mid-morning, after a light bite, rather than right when they wake up on an empty stomach. It's best to avoid coffee in the late afternoon and evening, since caffeine lingers in the body for many hours and can affect sleep. A reference point is to stop coffee at least 6–8 hours before bedtime.

Why does drinking coffee on an empty stomach feel unsettling?

Coffee is acidic and can stimulate the stomach to produce digestive juices. When the stomach is empty, a queasy, unsettled feeling or slightly shaky hands appear more easily, especially in people who are naturally sensitive. Drinking coffee after a light meal or alongside some food usually makes the stomach more comfortable.

Does afternoon coffee cause sleeplessness?

Caffeine takes many hours for the body to metabolize, so a late-afternoon cup can still be at work when you go to bed. For people who sleep poorly or are caffeine-sensitive, coffee after around 2–3pm can make sleep shallower and harder to fall into. If you tend to toss and turn, try moving your last cup earlier.

Is there a way to cut back on coffee and still stay alert?

Besides coffee, you can alternate with green tea, warm herbal tea, or simply a cup of warm water and a few minutes of light movement mid-session. Getting enough sleep, eating a balanced breakfast, and staying hydrated are also foundations that help the body maintain natural alertness, rather than depending entirely on caffeine.

Who should be cautious with coffee?

People with heart or blood pressure concerns, a sensitive stomach, or a tendency toward anxiety, as well as pregnant and breastfeeding women and people who sleep poorly, should be cautious and consult a doctor about a suitable amount of caffeine. For these groups, reducing the dose or choosing a lower-caffeine drink is often the gentler option.
