Eating Less Meat, Lacking Protein? How to Make Up With Plant Protein in Vietnamese Meals

IKI Health Coach Team· Updated 2026-08-10· 15 min read
Quick Answer

Eating less meat doesn't mean lacking protein. Protein is found in many foods besides meat: fish, eggs, milk and yogurt, tofu, beans, nuts, and even whole grains. A normal healthy adult needs about 0.8-1g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day; a person weighing 60kg needs around 50-60g. The real issue isn't cutting out meat — it's whether your meal is properly balanced. Cutting meat and eating only rice with boiled vegetables is the kind of eating that easily falls short on protein.

The question does eating less meat mean lacking protein comes up almost immediately whenever someone decides to cut back on meat — whether for health, cost, religious reasons, or simply because eating a lot of meat feels too heavy. Family members will ask right away: "so where will you get your nutrients from?" That question isn't unreasonable, it's just based on an assumption that isn't quite right: that protein mainly comes from meat.

In reality, protein is found in nearly every food group. Traditional Vietnamese meals already include plenty of non-meat protein sources: tofu, various beans, fish, eggs, peanuts and sesame. The issue isn't cutting out meat — it's whether you replace it with something else. This article answers specifically: how much protein you need, where to get it, how to build your meal, and what signs indicate you're falling short.

Quick answer: it depends on how you plan your meals

There are two very different ways of "eating less meat," and the results are quite different too.

The first type — cutting meat but substituting it. You skip the braised pork but add tofu simmered in tomato sauce, a bowl of bean soup, eggs in the morning, and sprinkle peanuts and sesame on vegetables. The meal still has enough protein, and often even more fiber than before. This is a style of eating many Vietnamese people are already familiar with from vegetarian days on the 1st and 15th of the lunar month.

The second type — cutting meat without replacing it with anything. The meal is left with just white rice, boiled vegetables, fish sauce, and a thin soup. This is the type that easily falls short on protein, and it tends to happen in two groups: people new to vegetarian eating who don't yet know how to plan meals, and older adults who eat less and less because they don't feel like cooking.

In other words, meat isn't the only protein source — just the most convenient one — easy to buy, easy to cook, densely packed into a small piece. When you remove it, you need to replace it with a few sources instead of just one, and pay a bit more attention when planning meals. That's really all there is to it.

Everyone has a different baseline constitution and activity level, so needs vary from person to person. If you're thinking of cutting back on meat and want to know what your body needs to watch out for, try the 90-second constitution check to get a more specific starting point than following generic advice.

How much protein you need each day

The commonly used reference level for a normally active adult is about 0.8 to 1 gram of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. More specifically:

  • 50kg person: about 40-50g of protein per day.
  • 60kg person: about 50-60g.
  • 70kg person: about 56-70g.

Some groups need more than this baseline: people who exercise regularly, older adults wanting to maintain muscle mass, people recovering from illness, and pregnant or breastfeeding women. On the other hand, people with kidney conditions need a protein amount specifically determined by their doctor, and should absolutely not increase it based on articles found online.

To picture what 50-60g of protein looks like in practice, here's the approximate protein content of some familiar servings:

  • 1 chicken egg: about 6-7g.
  • 1 piece of tofu, about 100g: about 8-10g.
  • 1 bowl of cooked black beans or lentils: about 15g.
  • 1 piece of fish, about 100g: about 18-20g.
  • 1 cup of yogurt: about 4-5g.
  • 1 handful of nuts, about 30g: about 5-7g.
  • 1 bowl of brown rice: about 5g.

Looking at this table reveals something interesting: with just two eggs in the morning, a piece of tofu, and a bowl of beans during the day, plus protein scattered through rice and vegetables, you're already close to the target. No meat needed at all. The article How Much Protein You Need Each Day has a more detailed table.

Protein isn't neatly packed into a plate of meat. It's spread across the whole meal — we just tend to only count the pieces of meat.

How plant protein differs from animal protein

There are real differences, but they're much smaller than commonly claimed.

Amino acid composition. Animal protein usually contains a full, well-balanced set of essential amino acids. Most plant sources are slightly low in one or two amino acids — for example, grains tend to be low in lysine, while legumes tend to be low in methionine. Soybeans and soy products in particular have a fairly complete amino acid profile, which is why tofu and soy milk are pillars of many vegetarian diets.

Absorption. Animal protein is generally absorbed a bit more easily. With plant protein, preparation method makes a significant difference: soaking beans before cooking, cooking thoroughly, or using fermented forms like fermented soybean paste and tempeh all help the body use them better.

What comes along with it. This is the most notable point. A piece of tofu comes with fiber, magnesium, and no cholesterol; a piece of red meat comes with easily absorbed iron but also saturated fat. Neither source is absolutely superior — the sensible approach is to include both if you're not fully vegetarian. The article Plant Protein and Animal Protein compares them in more detail.

Essential amino acids and how to combine sources

You may have heard the advice that vegetarians must combine rice with beans in the same meal to get enough protein. This is an outdated view that has since been revised: the body maintains a circulating pool of amino acids, so combining sources over the course of a whole day is enough — there's no need to combine them in a single meal.

Still, traditional pairings remain worth using because they're delicious and convenient:

  • Rice with beans — rice with black beans, rice with mung beans, sticky rice with beans.
  • Bread with peanuts or sesame — bread dipped in sesame salt, peanut butter.
  • Cereal with milk or nut milk — oats cooked with soy milk.
  • Vegetables with nuts — salad topped with cashews or pumpkin seeds.
  • Rice noodles or pho with tofu and bean sprouts — a very familiar style at Vietnamese vegetarian eateries.

The simplest practical principle: try to have at least three different protein sources each day. No further calculation needed.

List of non-meat protein sources for Vietnamese meals

Here are sources that are easy to find at Vietnamese markets, listed by convenience:

  • Tofu. Cheap, widely available, can be prepared a dozen ways — simmered in tomato sauce, crispy fried, steamed, cooked in soup, braised in fermented soybean paste. If people could choose only one protein source to replace meat, many would choose this. See also Is Eating Tofu Good for You.
  • Eggs. If you follow a lacto-ovo vegetarian diet, this is the neatest and most complete protein source, suitable for breakfast.
  • Beans and lentils. Black beans, mung beans, red beans, chickpeas, lentils. Cooking a big batch at the start of the week and dividing it into containers for the fridge is the most time-saving approach.
  • Fish and seafood. For those who only cut red meat but aren't vegetarian, fish is the most natural substitute — providing enough protein along with omega-3s.
  • Milk, yogurt, cheese. Convenient for snacks, especially for older adults eating less.
  • Nuts and seeds. Peanuts, sesame, cashews, almonds, pumpkin seeds. Vietnamese cuisine already has peanut-sesame salt — a simple dish that's rich in protein.
  • Whole grains. Brown rice, oats, buckwheat. The protein per serving isn't high, but since they're eaten in large quantities, the total adds up meaningfully.
  • Soybeans and soy products. Soy milk, tofu, tempeh, young soybeans. This group has the most complete amino acid profile among plant sources.
  • Protein-rich vegetables. Broccoli, jute leaves, peas, mushrooms. Not a main source, but they add up meaningfully over the course of a day.
  • Plant protein powder. For days when you don't have time to cook, or for long-term vegetarians who find it hard to reach their target amount. A serving of plant protein mixed with water or nut milk is a quick way to fill the protein gap for breakfast or a snack — used alongside a meal, not as a replacement for one.

Signs your meals are falling short on protein

The body doesn't send one clear signal for low protein, but there are a few signs worth noting when they appear together and persist:

  • Getting hungry again quickly after a meal. A meal high in carbs and low in protein often leaves you hungry again within an hour or two.
  • Gradually softening muscles and weakening strength even without a change in body weight — this sign is more noticeable in people over 50.
  • More hair loss, brittle nails, dry skin that persists over time.
  • Small wounds healing more slowly than usual.
  • Frequent tiredness and low stamina during everyday activities.
  • Constant snacking between meals.

To be clear: these signs aren't specific to low protein alone and can come from many other causes. They're only worth using as a prompt to review what you've eaten over the past few weeks. If the condition persists, see a doctor for a proper assessment instead of self-diagnosing. The article Signs Your Body May Be Low on Protein analyzes each sign in more detail.

Four other nutrients to watch when eating less meat

Ironically, when cutting back on meat, protein often isn't the biggest concern. These four nutrients are more likely to fall short:

  • Vitamin B12. Found almost exclusively in animal-derived foods. Long-term vegetarians need to supplement from fortified foods or supplements based on professional advice — this is a point not to take lightly. See Vitamin B12 for Vegetarians.
  • Iron. Iron from plants is absorbed less efficiently than iron from meat. Simple tip: pair with a vitamin C source — squeeze lemon over vegetables, eat fruit after meals — and avoid drinking strong tea right after eating. Details in Iron-Rich Foods.
  • Zinc. Found in pumpkin seeds, cashews, beans, and whole grains. Soaking beans and nuts before preparing them helps improve absorption.
  • Calcium. For those who also cut out dairy, pay attention to calcium-set tofu, dark leafy greens, sesame, and calcium-fortified nut milk. See Calcium Supplementation for Vegetarians.

Knowing these four names helps you avoid most of the risks of cutting meat without a plan.

How to build a protein-rich Vietnamese meal that's still light

You don't need to change your eating style entirely — just adjust the proportions. Here's an easy-to-remember formula for a family meal:

  1. One clear main protein dish. Braised tofu, braised fish, steamed egg, mushroom-stuffed tofu — there should be one dish everyone can take a proper portion of, not just a few small pieces scattered in a stir-fried vegetable dish.
  2. One soup with protein. Tofu and tomato soup, crab soup with jute leaves, mung bean soup. A protein-containing soup is a quiet way to significantly raise the total protein of a meal.
  3. Vegetables take up most of the plate. Boiled, lightly stir-fried, or raw, depending on the season.
  4. Moderate carbs, preferring some whole grain. Gradually mixing brown rice into white rice is the least resisted way to make the switch within a family.
  5. A sprinkle of nuts or peanut-sesame salt on top. Adds flavor as well as extra protein and healthy fats.

For breakfast and snacks — the two meals where protein is most often overlooked — keep a few options ready: boiled eggs, yogurt, a handful of nuts, soy milk, or a quick glass of plant protein for rushed mornings.

A sample 3-day menu

Day 1 — fish, no red meat. Breakfast: two boiled eggs, whole-grain bread, a banana. Lunch: rice, braised mackerel, jute leaf soup, boiled water spinach. Snack: a cup of yogurt. Dinner: rice noodles with grilled tofu, plenty of raw vegetables, a bit of roasted peanuts.

Day 2 — fully vegetarian. Breakfast: oats cooked with soy milk, chia seeds and banana added. Lunch: brown rice mixed with white rice, tofu simmered in tomato sauce, mung bean soup, boiled broccoli. Snack: a handful of cashews and almonds. Dinner: red bean porridge or vegetarian mushroom noodle soup, with tofu skin added.

Day 3 — a busy day, quick cooking. Breakfast: a glass of plant protein mixed with nut milk and half a banana. Lunch: takeout rice box with fried tofu and stir-fried vegetables, plus a boiled egg. Snack: yogurt and a few nuts. Dinner: quick tofu and tomato soup, boiled vegetables, rice.

All three days sit around the 50-60g protein mark without any red meat. What they have in common is that each meal has one clear protein source, and the sources rotate throughout the day.

Time-saving tip

Cook a large batch of beans on the weekend and divide it into frozen containers. Each meal, pull out one container for an instant protein source without spending time soaking and cooking. This tip is widely used by families who've been eating vegetarian for a long time.

Common misconceptions

"Not eating meat makes you weak." Health depends on the overall diet, not on one food group. Many long-term vegetarians still work and exercise normally. What actually makes people weak is usually eating too little total energy without a plan, not the absence of meat.

"Eating lots of vegetables is enough." Vegetables are important, but their protein content is low. A meal that's all vegetables with no tofu, eggs, or beans is the type of meal most likely to fall short on protein.

"Tofu has a lot of hormones, men shouldn't eat it." This is a common misconception. The compounds in soy are different from human hormones, and typical daily consumption isn't a concern for healthy people. The article Soy and Female Hormones covers this in more detail.

"Just drink protein powder and you're done." Protein powder only addresses the protein aspect, while a real meal brings fiber, vitamins, minerals, and enjoyment too. It's a helpful tool for busy times, not a foundation.

"More protein is always better." Going well beyond your needs offers no extra benefit, and people with kidney conditions need protein amounts as directed by their doctor. Enough is enough.

Who should be more cautious when cutting meat

For most healthy adults, cutting back on meat is safe as long as meals are properly planned. But a few groups should consult a nutrition expert or doctor before making major changes:

  • Young children and growing children — their protein needs per kilogram of body weight are higher than adults'.
  • Pregnant and breastfeeding women.
  • People over 65, since muscle mass gradually declines with age and protein is an important factor in preserving it. See also Protein for People Over 50 to Preserve Muscle Mass.
  • People with underlying health conditions, especially kidney disease, or those on a doctor-prescribed diet.
  • People doing high-intensity training or heavy physical labor.
  • People recovering from a recent illness.

For these groups, cutting back on meat is still feasible — it just requires a more specific plan rather than doing it by feel.

From the IKI community

Within the IKI community, the group that most often switches to eating less meat isn't vegetarians for religious reasons, but middle-aged people who find that meat-heavy meals make them feel heavy and bloated. What they often share is that the early stage felt lacking because they only knew to cut back without knowing what to replace it with; once they rearranged their meals to include one clear main protein dish each time, their sense of energy returned to normal. This is a personal experience of changing eating habits, and individual experiences may vary.

Conclusion: cutting meat is a choice, not a trade-off

To answer the original question simply: eating less meat does not mean lacking protein. What matters is whether your meals are properly balanced. Make sure each main meal has one clear protein source, use at least three different protein sources each day, and keep an eye on four nutrients that can easily fall short: B12, iron, zinc, and calcium.

You also don't need to choose between two extremes. Many people settle in the middle: cutting red meat, keeping fish and eggs, increasing beans and vegetables. This is a flexible eating style that's easy to sustain long-term and doesn't disrupt family meals — the article Flexible Vegetarianism (Flexitarian) describes exactly how to do it.

Since protein needs depend on each person's weight, age, and activity level, a general suggestion can't replace understanding yourself. Frequently asked questions

Does eating less meat mean lacking protein?

Not necessarily. Protein is found in fish, eggs, milk, yogurt, tofu, beans, nuts, and even whole grains, so a meal can be fully protein-adequate without much meat. Low protein intake usually happens when someone cuts meat without replacing it with any other protein source, eating mainly rice, boiled vegetables, and soup broth.

How much protein does a 60kg person need each day?

A commonly used reference level for a normally active adult is about 0.8-1g per kilogram of body weight, or about 50-60g of protein per day for a 60kg person. People who exercise a lot, older adults wanting to preserve muscle mass, and pregnant or breastfeeding women usually need more and should consult a nutrition expert.

Is plant protein inferior to animal protein?

Not in value, just different in structure. Most plant protein sources are slightly low in one or two essential amino acids, but when eaten in a varied way throughout the day — such as rice with beans, or bread with peanuts — the sources complement each other. Soybeans and soy products already have a fairly complete amino acid profile.

Do I need to eat rice with beans in the same meal to get enough protein?

No. The view that protein must be combined within the same meal is outdated. The body maintains a circulating pool of amino acids, so simply eating a variety of foods over the course of the day is enough. This makes meal planning much easier.

Is plant protein powder necessary?

Not required. If your meals already regularly include tofu, beans, eggs, or fish, you don't need to add it. Protein powder is helpful on busy days when you don't have time to cook, for long-term vegetarians who find it hard to reach their target amount, or for older adults eating small amounts. This is a dietary supplement used alongside meals, not a replacement for a complete meal.
