What Is a Flexitarian Diet? How to Eat Less Meat Without Giving It Up Entirely

IKI Health Coach Team· Updated 2026-08-09· 14 min read
Quick answer

A flexitarian diet is a way of eating built on a plant-based foundation — vegetables, legumes, whole grains, and nuts make up most of the plate — while still including meat, fish, eggs, and dairy in moderation when needed or wanted. There's no hard rule about how many meat-free meals you need; you can start with 1–2 plant-based meals per week and build up gradually. The strength of this approach is that it's easy to sustain long-term and easier to fit into a Vietnamese family meal table than full vegetarianism.

There's a huge gap between two choices that many people assume are the only options: either eat meat as usual, or become a full-time vegetarian. People who want to eat more vegetables and less meat, but aren't ready (or don't need) to give up meat and fish entirely, often don't know where they fit — and end up not changing anything at all.

Flexitarian eating is exactly that middle ground. It's a way of eating that's been talked about more in Vietnam in recent years, and interestingly, it isn't really new to Vietnamese people at all: many families already eat vegetarian on the 1st and 15th of the lunar month, and eat plant-heavy meals a few days a week — it just hasn't been named as a systematic approach before.

This article explains what a flexitarian diet is, how it differs from other vegetarian styles, how to start without falling short on nutrients, and a seven-day sample menu built from ingredients you can find at a Vietnamese market.

What a flexitarian diet is

The word flexitarian combines flexible and vegetarian, coined and popularized by American dietitian Dawn Jackson Blatner in the 2000s. A concise definition: plant foods form the foundation of your meals, animal foods are still used in moderation, and there are no absolute boundaries.

Three features set it apart from other ways of eating:

First, there are no forbidden foods. You don't "break a rule" by eating a meal with meat in it. This sounds small, but it's actually the main reason this approach is so easy to sustain long-term: there's no sense of failure, so there's no excuse to give up.

Second, the focus is on ratio, not category. Instead of asking "is this food allowed or not," the question is "what percentage of today's plate is plant-based." A practical target for beginners: about two-thirds of the plate should be vegetables, legumes, and whole grains; the rest can be animal protein if you have any.

Third, it's measured weekly, not per meal. One barbecue dinner with meat doesn't ruin anything, as long as your overall week still leans plant-based.

This approach is clearly different from full-time vegetarianism (eliminating meat and fish entirely), veganism (also eliminating eggs, dairy, honey), and the pescatarian diet (no meat, but fish is fine). If you're curious about the boundaries between these approaches, the article eating vegetarian with balanced nutrition has a more detailed comparison.

Why many people choose this way of eating

Much easier to sustain

This is the biggest advantage, and also the least talked about. No matter how good a diet is, it's worthless if you give up after three weeks. Flexitarian eating doesn't ask you to refuse food at a wedding, doesn't force you to cook separately at family meals, and doesn't create friction with family members. That's exactly why it can last year after year.

Fits a Vietnamese meal table and budget

The traditional Vietnamese meal table is already close to this model: rice, a bowl of vegetable soup, a plate of boiled or stir-fried greens, a small portion of protein, plus tofu or peanuts. Shifting to a flexitarian diet is mostly just changing the ratio, not changing your kitchen. On top of that, beans, peanuts, sesame, and tofu are all significantly cheaper than meat and fish — meal costs usually go down, not up.

More vegetables and fiber

As the animal protein portion shrinks, the empty space on the plate naturally fills up with vegetables and legumes. This brings higher fiber, vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants — things that every nutrition survey shows urban Vietnamese are currently not getting enough of.

Lighter on the environment and on your stomach

Plant-based food production uses fewer resources. And on a very everyday level: many people report that after a few weeks of cutting back on fried food and red meat, dinner feels lighter and sleep comes easier. The article a light dinner that's easy to digest goes deeper into this connection.

Four levels so you can choose your starting point

You don't need to jump straight into the hardest level. Choose one that fits your current rhythm and build up gradually.

Level 1 — First-timer (about 2 plant-based days per week). Pick two fixed days each week, say Monday and Thursday, and eat entirely plant-based. Keep the rest of the days as usual. This is the level most people can start this very week without any preparation.

Level 2 — Plant-based breakfasts (7 meat-free breakfasts per week). Switch all breakfasts to plant-based: oatmeal, nut milk, peanut butter on bread, sticky rice with beans, vegetarian bún riêu. Breakfast is the easiest meal to change since it's usually eaten alone with little ceremony.

Level 3 — Vegetarian on weekdays (about 10–14 meat-free meals per week). Weekday meals lean plant-based, while weekends with family are more relaxed. At this level, you need to start paying attention to designing protein into your plant-based meals.

Level 4 — Mostly plant-based (only 1–2 meals with animal protein per week). Close to full-time vegetarianism but still leaves the door open. At this level, you should read closely the nutrients section below, especially B12 and iron.

A practical tip: don't skip more than two levels at once. People who start at level 1 and move to level 2 after a month usually go further than those who jump straight to level 4 in the first week.

Four nutrients to watch when reducing animal protein

Flexitarian eating is safer than full-time vegetarianism from a nutrition standpoint, but "safer" doesn't mean you can ignore it entirely.

Protein — swap the source, don't leave it empty

The most common mistake beginners make: taking the meat off the plate without replacing it with anything. The result is a meal of just rice and vegetables, which leaves you hungry fast, and reaching for snacks two hours later.

The rule: every meal still needs a clear protein source. In a plant-based meal, that source can be tofu, silken tofu, dried bean curd sheets, black beans, mung beans, lentils, chickpeas, tempeh, peanuts, sesame, nuts, or protein-rich grains like oats and quinoa.

For busy people or those training who need more protein, plant-based protein powder is a quick fix for meals you don't have time to cook — mixed into your morning nut milk or taken after a workout. It doesn't replace a meal, but it fills the gap that a busy schedule creates.

How to calculate your daily protein needs is covered in how much protein do you need per day, and the difference between the two protein sources is analyzed in plant protein vs. animal protein.

Iron — remember the vitamin C trick

Iron from plants is absorbed less efficiently than iron from red meat. This isn't a problem if you know two simple tricks: add a vitamin C source to the same meal (squeeze lime onto vegetables, have citrus or guava for dessert, stir-fry with tomato and bell pepper) and avoid strong tea or coffee right after a meal.

Women of reproductive age need to pay closer attention since their iron needs are higher. A list of iron sources in Vietnamese meals is in iron-rich foods.

Vitamin B12 — found only in animal sources

This is a nutrient that plants essentially don't provide. Fortunately, flexitarians still eat eggs, dairy, fish, and meat during the week, so the risk is much lower than for vegans. But if you're at level 4 (only 1–2 animal-based meals per week), be sure to read vitamin B12 for vegetarians and consider talking to your doctor about periodic testing.

Omega-3, zinc, and calcium

Omega-3: if you're cutting back on fish, increase flaxseed, chia seeds, and walnuts — see plant-based omega-3. Zinc: found in pumpkin seeds, cashews, legumes, and whole grains; soaking beans before cooking improves absorption. Details at zinc and zinc-rich foods. Calcium: tofu (made with calcium sulfate), sesame, dark leafy greens, calcium-fortified nut milk — see also calcium supplementation for vegetarians.

A seven-day sample menu at level 3

The framework below has about 12 plant-based meals out of 21 total. Swap dishes based on the season and what's available near you.

Monday (fully plant-based) — Breakfast: oatmeal with nut milk, banana, and black sesame. Lunch: brown rice, tofu in tomato sauce, pumpkin soup, boiled water spinach. Dinner: vegetarian bún riêu with plenty of vegetables.

Tuesday — Breakfast: whole-grain bread with peanut butter, one guava. Lunch: rice, braised fish, canh rau ngót soup, boiled mustard greens. Dinner: chickpea and vegetable salad, a handful of nuts.

Wednesday — Breakfast: sticky rice with mung beans, or nut milk mixed with plant protein powder. Lunch: rice, lemongrass-fried tofu, sour mushroom soup, boiled sweet potato greens. Dinner: red bean porridge, steamed tofu.

Thursday (fully plant-based) — Breakfast: banana-avocado smoothie with nut milk. Lunch: rice, braised lentils with carrot and potato, canh rau dền soup. Dinner: mixed vegetable noodles with pan-seared tofu.

Friday — Breakfast: two boiled eggs, one slice of bread. Lunch: rice, braised lean meat, mustard green soup, a large plate of vegetables. Dinner: pumpkin and cashew soup, whole-grain bread.

Saturday — Breakfast: phở or bún with plenty of vegetables. Lunch: rice, braised shrimp, vegetable soup, mushroom-stuffed tofu. Dinner: light rice, stir-fried mushrooms with vegetables, braised dried bean curd sheets with pepper.

Sunday — Family meal, eat freely. Keep just one rule: half the plate is still vegetables.

Six common mistakes beginners make

1. Eating "plant-based" but all fried food. Deep-fried tofu, vegetarian mock meat, instant vegetarian noodles — still plant-based, but not necessarily healthy. Prioritize boiling, steaming, braising, and stir-frying with little oil.

2. Relying on packaged vegetarian foods. Vegetarian sausage rolls, hot dogs, and mock meats are often high in salt, starch, and additives. Fine occasionally for variety, but not as the foundation of your meals.

3. Cutting protein without replacing it. Mentioned above, and this is mistake number one.

4. Eating too little and thinking it's healthy. Plant-based meals often have less energy density; if you don't increase your portion of starchy vegetables, you'll easily get hungry between meals and snack to compensate.

5. Thinking you have to be perfect. One meal with meat doesn't erase a whole week of effort. The whole spirit of this approach is that it's not perfect.

6. Changing too fast and upsetting your stomach. A sudden increase in fiber can cause bloating in the first few days. Increase gradually, drink enough water, and if your stomach feels off, read more at bloating and indigestion after eating.

Flexitarian eating within Vietnamese daily life

Vietnamese people already have the foundation for this way of eating without needing to learn it from anywhere else.

Eating vegetarian on the 1st and 15th of the lunar month already amounts to two plant-based days a month. If you already keep this practice, you're already at the starting level — just add one more day during the week to reach level 1. The article eating vegetarian on the 1st and 15th with enough nutrients has suggestions so those days don't leave you hungry or short on protein.

Multi-generational family meals are the trickiest part, since you don't want to cook separately or inconvenience anyone. The smoothest approach: keep the shared meal table as it is, just add one tofu or legume dish to the table, and adjust the ratio on your own plate yourself. No need to announce anything.

Eating out. Everyday rice shops always have tofu, boiled vegetables, and soup; you can ask for extra vegetables with bún or phở; and vegetarian restaurants are increasingly common in cities. When entertaining guests, just eat normally — that's exactly why this approach is called "flexible."

The Eastern medicine perspective also aligns well with this direction: balanced meals, plenty of seasonal vegetables, less heavy fried food, warm and moderate eating. If you're interested in choosing foods based on your constitution, the two articles 5 body constitutions in Eastern medicine and eating by season and constitution are a good place to start.

Who should be cautious

This approach suits most healthy adults, but a few groups should talk to a doctor or dietitian before making major changes:

  • Pregnant and breastfeeding women (iron, B12, calcium, and protein needs are all higher).
  • Children and teenagers who are still growing.
  • Older adults at risk of muscle loss — see protein for people over 50 to preserve muscle mass.
  • People with anemia, iron deficiency, or chronic conditions that require nutrition monitoring.
  • People with kidney disease who have been given a specific protein target by their doctor.

For these groups, flexitarian eating can still be applied, it just needs to be planned more carefully rather than cut back on your own.

Start this week

If you'd like to try it, here's the simplest possible four-week plan:

  • Week 1: choose one day a week to eat entirely plant-based. Just one day.
  • Week 2: keep that day, and add switching breakfast to plant-based three times.
  • Week 3: increase to two meat-free days, and start paying attention to a clear protein source in each plant-based meal.
  • Week 4: look back — how do you feel, which meals were easy, which were hard, which dishes you actually enjoyed. Keep what works, drop what doesn't.

No need to weigh or measure anything, no need for complicated apps. What matters most is consistency and a bit of self-observation.

Frequently asked questions

What is a flexitarian diet?

Flexitarian combines flexible and vegetarian. It's a way of eating that prioritizes plant-based foods without completely eliminating meat, fish, eggs, or dairy. A flexitarian can eat plant-based for a few meals or days a week, while eating meat in moderate amounts the rest of the time.

How does flexitarian eating differ from full-time vegetarianism?

Full-time vegetarianism eliminates meat and fish entirely (and for vegans, also eggs, dairy, and honey) at every meal. Flexitarian eating sets no absolute boundaries: you gradually reduce animal protein, keep a small portion, and adjust based on circumstances like parties, business trips, or family meals.

Can flexitarian eating lead to nutrient deficiencies?

It carries less risk than full-time vegetarianism since you still have animal protein, heme iron, and vitamin B12. However, if you reduce meat and fish without replacing them with legumes, nuts, eggs, or plant protein, you can still fall short on protein. The rule is that every meal still needs a clear protein source — swap the source, don't leave it empty.

How should you start a flexitarian diet?

The easiest way is to pick two fixed days a week as plant-based days, or switch your entire breakfast to plant-based. Keep the rest of your meals as usual for the first few weeks, then increase gradually once you're used to it. Starting small and sustaining it is better than making a big change and giving up after ten days.

Is flexitarian eating suitable for people who work out?

Yes, as long as you account for your total daily protein intake. People who train need more protein than sedentary people, so plant-based meals need to be planned more carefully: tofu, legumes, tempeh, nuts, or adding plant protein powder if you can't eat enough in time.

Does eating vegetarian on the 1st and 15th of the lunar month count as flexitarian eating?

In essence, it's very close. Many Vietnamese families already eat plant-based two days a month as a traditional practice — that's naturally a form of flexitarian eating. The difference is that the flexitarian approach focuses more on nutrition and consistent frequency, rather than being tied only to specific holidays.
