Eat Slowly, Chew Well: A Small Habit, Big Rewards

IKI Health Coach Team· Updated 2026-07-25· 5 min read
Quick answer

Digestion starts in the mouth: chewing well breaks food into smaller pieces and mixes it evenly with saliva, so your stomach and gut can work far more comfortably. Eating slowly also gives your brain enough time to register fullness — usually around 20 minutes — so you stop at the right moment instead of overeating. A simple way to practice: put your chopsticks down between bites, chew each bite more than usual, avoid eating in front of a screen, and set aside at least 20 minutes to sit and eat properly.

A ten-minute lunch in front of the computer screen, dinner eaten while scrolling on the phone — we're eating faster than ever, and often can't even recall what we just ate. Meanwhile, there's an old-fashioned habit our grandparents always reminded us of, one that both modern science and Japanese food philosophy hold in high regard: slow down and chew thoroughly. It sounds simple enough to be easily dismissed — but this may be the smallest change with the biggest payoff at the dinner table.

Digestion begins in the mouth, not the stomach

Teeth grind food into small pieces, saliva soaks in and starts breaking down starches right there in the mouth. The more thoroughly you chew, the lighter the workload passed down to your stomach. On the other hand, swallowing large bites too quickly means your stomach has to strain to do the job your teeth were supposed to handle — contributing to the bloating and sluggish digestion after meals that many people complain about. Eating slowly and chewing well is the fairest way to "share the workload" across your whole digestive system.

Your brain needs 20 minutes to know you're full

The fullness signal traveling from stomach to brain isn't instant — it usually takes about twenty minutes. Eating too fast means that by the time your brain finally says "that's enough," you've long since eaten more than you needed. That's why people who eat quickly often end up uncomfortably stuffed after meals, while those who eat slowly can stop at a comfortable, just-right point — in the same spirit as the Japanese practice of eating until about 80% full (hara hachi bu). For anyone working on maintaining a healthy weight, eating pace is a free lever that few people actually use.

A slow meal is also a break for your mind

Eating slowly isn't just about the stomach. Twenty minutes of sitting down to eat properly — noticing the sweetness of the rice, the crunch of the vegetables, the aroma of a hot soup — is a real pause in a busy day, something scrolling on your phone can never replace. Many people notice that once they stop eating in front of a screen, the same old meal suddenly tastes so much better. Being fully present for a meal like this is also its own kind of active relaxation — well suited to anyone who feels wound up tight all day.

Tips for eating slower in a fast-paced life

  • Put your chopsticks down between bites: pick up — chew — put down — then pick up again. This one motion alone slows a meal down considerably.
  • Chew more than usual: no need to count chews mechanically; just chew until the food is really well broken down before swallowing.
  • Keep screens away from the table: eating in front of a screen is a mindless kind of eating — fast, excessive, and forgettable.
  • Set aside at least 20 minutes for your main meal: schedule a proper lunch break instead of squeezing food in between two meetings.
  • Start with one meal a day: pick a family dinner as your first "slow meal," then gradually let it spread to other meals.

No tools needed, no extra cost — just slow down a little, and in return you get a lighter stomach, a tastier meal, and a rested mind. That's a bargain worth starting with tonight's dinner.

Frequently asked questions

How many times should I chew each bite?

There's no fixed number that works for everyone and every dish. A simple rule of thumb: chew until the food is smooth and well broken down before swallowing — rice and vegetables usually need more chews than a soup or porridge. Counting chews is only useful as a reminder for the first few days; after that, it becomes natural.

Does eating slowly help with maintaining weight?

Yes, it can clearly help. Eating slowly gives your brain enough time to register fullness, so you tend to stop at a just-right amount instead of eating past your needs. A number of studies have found that people who eat slowly tend to eat less during a meal while still feeling satisfied. Of course, overall diet and physical activity still play the biggest role.

My work only allows a short lunch break — how can I eat slowly?

Try to separate eating time from everything else: 15–20 minutes just for eating, no screens, small bites, and thorough chewing. If time is really tight, prepare meals that are easy to eat slowly, like a vegetable-heavy lunch box, instead of fast food — and make up for it with a relaxed dinner at home.
