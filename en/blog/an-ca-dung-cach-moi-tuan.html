How to Eat Enough Fish Each Week

IKI Health Coach Team· Updated 2026-08-05· 9 min read
Quick answer

Adults should aim for about 2-3 fish meals per week, alternating between fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, pangasius, herring) and white fish (snakehead, tilapia). Choose fish with clear eyes, bright red gills, and firm bodies; cook by steaming, gentle simmering, or quick pan-searing to preserve omega-3s.

Fish has long been a familiar part of Vietnamese kitchens, from mom's clay-pot braised fish to the tangy fish soup of summer days. But when it comes to eating fish in a way that's both nutritionally adequate and fits today's busy pace, many people still wonder: how many meals a week is right, which type of fish to choose, and how to cook it without losing nutrients. This article gathers some practical, easy-to-apply suggestions for your family meals.

Why fish deserves a spot in your weekly menu

Fish is an easily digestible protein source with less tough connective tissue than red meat, making it suitable for all ages, from young children to older adults. Fish also contains omega-3 fatty acids, especially EPA and DHA, fats the body cannot produce enough of on its own and must obtain from food. This is also why nutrition experts often recommend alternating fish with other animal-protein meals, rather than relying solely on pork or beef as many families are used to.

Besides protein and fat, fish also carries minerals like iodine, zinc, and selenium, depending on the type. Sea fish tend to be richer in iodine than freshwater fish, while small fish eaten whole with bones, such as anchovies or round scad, are a notable source of natural calcium when prepared thoroughly. Overall, a menu with regular fish helps diversify meals, avoiding the trap of cycling through just a few repeated meat dishes each week — something busy working families easily fall into, as mentioned in the article preparing a week's worth of meals for working people.

How many fish meals a week is reasonable

For healthy adults, a commonly suggested amount in nutrition guidelines is about two to three fish meals per week, each roughly a palm-sized portion. This number isn't fixed for everyone — it depends on total weekly protein intake, habits around eating other meats and seafood, and each family's budget.

An easy way to plan is to divide the week into different protein groups: a few meat meals, a few fish meals, plus one or two meals of tofu or eggs, alongside colorful vegetable dishes. This kind of distribution keeps meals from feeling repetitive while making the most of each food group's strengths, similar to the protein grouping discussed in how much protein do you need each day. If your family has young children or older adults, prioritize white fish with small, easy-to-remove bones to reduce the risk of choking and make digestion easier.

For those watching their weight or wanting to cut back on red meat, fish meals can be increased slightly to three or four times a week, as long as total protein intake stays balanced and isn't concentrated in a single meal.

Telling fatty fish and white fish apart, which to prioritize

Fish is generally divided into two large groups based on fat content: fatty fish and white fish (also called lean fish). Fatty fish like salmon, mackerel, herring, pangasius, and short-bodied mackerel usually have fat distributed throughout the flesh, giving them a distinctive rich flavor and a higher omega-3 content. White fish like snakehead, tilapia, perch, and croaker have lower fat content and firmer flesh, suitable for those who want a lighter, less oily meal.

There's no need to choose one exclusively — it's best to alternate both throughout the week. You can use fatty fish for one or two meals to boost omega-3 intake, and white fish for soups, porridge, or light steaming. This combination also adds variety to the palate, avoiding the monotony of eating the same type of fish repeatedly. For those interested in learning more about types of dietary fat, see good fats and bad fats to understand why the omega-3 in fish is considered a fat group worth prioritizing in a daily menu.

For vegetarians or those who limit seafood, omega-3 can still partly come from flaxseed, chia seeds, and canola oil, as mentioned in plant-based omega-3, though the conversion rate into EPA and DHA is typically lower than eating fish directly.

How to choose fresh fish at Vietnamese markets

At traditional Vietnamese markets, fish is usually sold fresh or lightly iced, so observing with your eyes and hands is the simplest way to assess freshness. For whole fish, pay attention to a few things: clear, slightly bulging eyes that aren't sunken or cloudy; gills that are bright pinkish-red, not slimy or foul-smelling; scales that are firmly attached and shiny, without excessive flaking; and when pressed gently, the flesh should spring back quickly without leaving a dent.

Smell is also an important indicator. Fresh fish usually has a mild, characteristic smell of raw fish, without a strong foul odor or unpleasant ammonia scent. If buying pre-cut fish, choose pieces with natural coloring, not overly pale white or showing signs of being soaked in bleaching chemicals.

For frozen fish bought at supermarkets or food stores, check the expiration date carefully and whether the packaging is torn or shows unusual ice buildup. Storing fish properly after purchase matters just as much — refrigerate or cook fresh fish the same day, and avoid leaving it at room temperature too long. These basic food storage principles are covered in more detail in proper food storage, which applies to fish, meat, and other seafood as well.

A small tip from experienced home cooks: only buy enough fish for one or two meals, and avoid freezing it for too long, since long-frozen fish tends to lose some of its natural sweetness and texture.

Preparing fish properly to preserve nutrients

Preparation is a step that's often rushed but has a significant impact on the quality of the dish. When cleaning fish, remove the gills, intestines, and the dark membrane inside the belly, as these parts tend to cause a strong fishy smell and offer little nutritional value. Rinse the fish with clean water; you can rub a little salt or ginger gently over the body to reduce the fishy smell without soaking it too long, which could wash away some water-soluble nutrients.

For whole fish used in steaming or soups, making a few shallow cuts on the body helps it cook evenly and absorb seasoning better without needing to fry it first. For fish cut into pieces for braising or frying, let the surface dry naturally for a few minutes after rinsing so it holds its shape better during cooking instead of falling apart.

One thing to keep in mind: don't thaw fish with hot water or leave it at room temperature for too long, as this allows bacteria to grow quickly and affects freshness and taste. A safer way to thaw is to move fish from the freezer to the fridge the night before, or seal it in a bag and place it under cold running water if needed quickly.

Cooking methods that preserve omega-3 and nutrients

Cooking method has a considerable effect on preserving beneficial fatty acids in fish. In general, cooking at moderate temperatures for shorter times — steaming, gentle braising, quick pan-searing, or medium-heat grilling — tends to preserve nutrients better than deep-frying at high heat for a long time, since excessive heat and prolonged oil contact can alter some of the good fats in fish.

Ginger-scallion steamed fish, gently braised clay-pot fish, tangy fish soup, or pan-seared pangasius with a bit of olive oil are all cooking methods that preserve traditional flavor without requiring excessive oil. When marinating fish, familiar Vietnamese kitchen spices like ginger, turmeric, and lemongrass can add flavor while making the dish gentler on digestion, as suggested in Vietnamese spices: ginger, turmeric, lemongrass.

For fatty fish like salmon or mackerel, just a few minutes of searing on each side, seasoned with a little salt, pepper, and lime, makes for a well-rounded dish without elaborate preparation. For white fish, steaming with mushrooms, scallions, and tomatoes creates a natural sauce, reducing the need for extra oil or processed seasonings.

Avoid frying fish repeatedly in the same batch of oil, as oil reused many times can produce compounds that aren't good for long-term health. If you enjoy crispy fried fish, opt for pan-frying with a small amount of oil instead of deep-frying.

Pairing fish with vegetables throughout the week

A well-rounded fish meal isn't just about the fish itself, but also how it's paired with vegetables. Tangy fish soup usually includes tomatoes, okra, bean sprouts, and herbs; braised fish is often eaten with cucumber and raw greens; steamed fish pairs well with steamed leafy greens. Adding vegetables to a fish meal not only balances nutrition but also supports easier digestion after a protein-rich meal.

You can build a weekly menu by alternating: steamed fish with leafy greens early in the week, tangy fish soup with colorful vegetables midweek, and braised fish with raw greens or a light salad on the weekend. Ideas for adding variety and color to the vegetables served with fish are covered further in leafy greens every day, helping keep family meals from feeling repetitive.

To save cooking time during a busy week, you can prep fish and vegetables over the weekend, portion them into storage containers, and simply cook quickly on busy days. This kind of preparation also helps avoid the trap of choosing unhealthy fast food when hungry and short on time.

Fish for different members of the family

Young children starting solids or beginning to eat rice should start with white fish that has few small bones, thoroughly deboned and cooked until soft, avoiding fish with many fine bones that could cause choking. Older adults should also prioritize soft, easy-to-chew fish, which can be steamed or braised until tender for easier digestion, while also serving as a gentle protein source that helps maintain muscle mass over time, as mentioned in protein for people over 50 to maintain muscle mass.

For pregnant or breastfeeding women, fish remains a protein and omega-3 source worth keeping in the diet, but it's best to prioritize small fish or farmed fish with clear origins, and limit large predatory fish from higher up the food chain if their source is unclear, as these can accumulate certain heavy metals over their lifetime.

Those who exercise regularly or want to maintain muscle can slightly increase their fish intake for the week, combined with other protein sources like eggs, beans, and lean meat, so total daily protein needs are met without relying entirely on one food type.

Common mistakes when eating fish and how to avoid them

A common mistake is eating only a few familiar types of fish over a long period, which makes meals less varied and may miss out on nutrients specific to different fish types. Try switching between sea fish and freshwater fish, fatty fish and white fish depending on the season and market prices, to keep meals varied while staying budget-friendly — similar to the seasonal food choices mentioned in eating healthy on a budget.

Another mistake is over-preparing fish with too much oil or heavy sauces, turning what's naturally a light dish into something heavy. Additionally, leaving fish at room temperature too long before cooking, or freezing and thawing it repeatedly, is something to avoid, as it affects both freshness and food safety.

Finally, many people tend to skip reading the packaging information when buying frozen or pre-packaged fish at the supermarket. Taking a few seconds to check the expiration date, origin, and storage instructions on the packaging is a small habit worth keeping, as suggested in reading food labels.

Eating fish regularly each week doesn't need to be complicated — just a bit of care when choosing at the market, careful preparation, and gentle cooking can bring both delicious and balanced meals to your whole family. If there's no fish on this week's menu yet, try starting with a simple steamed fish dish, then gradually explore new cooking methods that suit your family's taste.

Do this today

  2. At the market, choose fish with bright red gills and flesh that springs back when pressed before buying.
  3. Tonight, steam or gently braise a fish dish instead of deep-frying.

Frequently asked questions

Is frozen fish less nutritious than fresh fish?

If frozen properly right after being caught, frozen fish still retains most of its nutrients, including omega-3. What matters is storing it at a stable temperature and not thawing and refreezing it repeatedly.

Is it okay to eat fish every day?

Eating fish daily isn't something you must avoid, but it's best to alternate with other protein sources like eggs, beans, and meat for more variety in meals. Rotating foods throughout the week is generally encouraged over repeating a single type.

When should young children start eating fish?

Many families begin introducing fish during the weaning stage, prioritizing white fish with few bones, thoroughly deboned and cooked until soft. Watch your child's reaction during the first few times to adjust if needed.
