Sleep & Evening Routine

What to Eat Before Bed for Better Sleep: 12 Easy-to-Find Options and Foods to Avoid

IKI Health Coach Team· Updated 2026-08-04· 14 min read
Quick Answer

If you feel hungry before bed, choose a small snack (about 150–250 kcal), warm, low in oil and low in sugar, and finish eating about 60–90 minutes before you get into bed. Easy-to-find options in Vietnam include banana, a small handful of almonds or pumpkin seeds, thin oatmeal porridge, unsweetened yogurt, kiwi, a cup of warm nut milk, or a cup of caffeine-free herbal tea. It's best to avoid late, oily dinners, spicy foods, alcohol, and coffee or strong tea after 3pm. Diet is only half the story — light exposure, screens, and a consistent bedtime make up the rest.

Here's a scene many people know well: it's 10:30pm, you lie down, clearly tired but your mind is still wide awake; your stomach feels unsettled — not quite hungry, but not full either. You get up, open the fridge, eat a little something, then toss and turn for another hour. The next morning you wake up feeling heavy, your stomach a bit bloated, and you tell yourself, "must have been the late-night snack."

That's why so many people search for what to eat before bed for better sleep. The short answer is: you don't have to go hungry, but your evening snack should meet three criteria — small, warm, and light. Along with that comes the right timing, and a few foods to actively avoid.

This article gathers the food groups commonly mentioned in general nutrition guidance, narrows them down to a list of items easy to buy at markets and supermarkets in Vietnam, along with specific portions, suggested meal ideas, and an evening routine you can try for 7 days. This is general health-care knowledge and is not a substitute for a doctor's advice.

Why dinner and evening snacks affect your sleep

Sleep doesn't begin the moment you turn off the light. It starts hours earlier, as your body gradually shifts from "working" mode into "resting" mode: body temperature drops slightly, heart rate slows, digestive activity eases. Your last meal of the day falls right in the middle of that transition, so it carries a fair amount of influence.

There are three easy-to-recognize ways this happens:

  • Feeling overly full or overly hungry. Eating too much right before bed forces your stomach to keep working when it should be resting — you feel bloated, find it hard to lie on your side, and are more prone to heartburn. On the other hand, going to bed with an empty, growling stomach can also wake you up in the middle of the night, especially if you ate dinner early and then exercised in the evening.
  • Raw materials for sleep-related messengers. The body uses an amino acid called tryptophan (found in food) as a building block to make serotonin, which is then converted into melatonin — the compound linked to your sleep-wake rhythm. Some foods also naturally contain a small amount of melatonin.
  • Blood sugar fluctuations. A dinner heavy in refined carbs and sweets can cause blood sugar to spike and then drop quickly overnight, and many people describe this as "waking up at 3am with a slightly racing heart."

What's worth noting is that none of these three factors require strict dieting. They simply call for choosing the right type and right amount for your last meal of the day. If you're struggling with lying awake unable to sleep, read more in Trouble Sleeping, Tossing and Turning, and Your Evening Routine for a fuller picture.

Three nutrient groups often mentioned in relation to sleep

When reading nutrition advice about sleep, you'll keep coming across a few names. Understand them once, and you'll be able to pick your own foods without needing to memorize a list.

Tryptophan — the upstream building block

Tryptophan is an essential amino acid, meaning the body can't produce it on its own and must get it from food. It's found in soybeans and soy products, pumpkin seeds, sesame seeds, oats, milk and yogurt, eggs, and fish. One detail few people notice: tryptophan enters the brain more easily when eaten alongside a small amount of healthy carbohydrate. That's why "a cup of warm milk with a few spoonfuls of oats" sounds simple but is actually a fairly sound combination.

Magnesium and potassium — two relaxation minerals

Magnesium is involved in hundreds of processes in the body, including regulating muscle and nervous system activity. People who often get leg cramps at night, or feel their calves tighten while lying down, are often advised to look at their magnesium and potassium intake. Easy sources include pumpkin seeds, almonds, cashews, dark leafy greens, bananas, sweet potatoes, and black beans. See Magnesium and Foods Rich in Magnesium for a more detailed list.

Natural melatonin in food

Some foods naturally contain small amounts of melatonin — the most well-known being cherries, followed by kiwi, grapes, tomatoes, brown rice, and a few types of nuts. The amount is not large, so don't expect it to replace good habits. Think of it as a small bonus that adds up alongside other things.

An important note

No single food "makes you sleepy" at the flip of a switch. Food only provides raw materials and helps the body feel comfortable; most of your sleep quality still comes from consistent timing, light exposure, and daytime stress levels. 12 foods to eat before bed, easy to find in Vietnam

The list below is arranged by how easy each item is to buy and prepare. Suggested portions are for an evening snack, not a full meal.

  1. Ripe banana (1 medium). Provides potassium and magnesium, along with a bit of easily digestible carbohydrate. A lifesaver when you're hungry at 9pm and don't want to cook. A well-ripened banana is gentler on the stomach than an unripe, astringent one.
  2. Almonds (10–15 pieces). Rich in magnesium and healthy fats. Eat them one at a time and chew well. Don't eat a big handful — nuts are very energy-dense.
  3. Pumpkin seeds (1 tablespoon). One of the best sources of tryptophan and magnesium among nuts and seeds, and quite affordable too.
  4. Thin oatmeal porridge (3–4 tablespoons of oats, cooked with water or nut milk). Warming, easy to digest, with slow-release carbs. Great for people who often feel a cold stomach in the evening. See more on how to eat oats the right way.
  5. Unsweetened yogurt (1 small cup). Provides a light amount of protein and probiotics. If your stomach is sensitive to cold, let the yogurt sit out of the fridge for 15–20 minutes before eating.
  6. Kiwi (1–2 pieces). Often mentioned in sleep-related articles, plus a good source of vitamin C and fiber.
  7. Warm nut milk (1 cup, 200ml, unsweetened or very lightly sweetened). Soy milk, almond milk, or lotus seed milk all work. You can make nut milk at home to control the sugar content.
  8. A slice of whole-grain bread with a thin spread of peanut butter. Combines slow-release carbs with protein and fat — filling without feeling heavy.
  9. Boiled sweet potato (half a medium piece). Provides potassium, fiber, and natural sweetness, enough to satisfy an evening sweet craving.
  10. Steamed silken tofu or a bowl of ginger tofu pudding with little sugar. Easy-to-digest plant protein, warming, and well-suited to Vietnamese taste.
  11. Boiled egg (1 piece). If you exercise in the evening and need extra protein without eating too much, a boiled egg is a neat, simple choice.
  12. A cup of warm, caffeine-free herbal tea. Not meant to "make you sleepy," but to create a closing point for the evening — holding a warm cup, dimming the lights, is a familiar signal to your body that the day is done. If you'd like a gentle herbal drink for this time of day, Thanh Huong Tea is made to be enjoyed warm in the evening; it's a dietary supplement meant to support your routine, not to replace medication.

You don't need to eat several items at once. One or two from this list is enough for an evening snack.

Foods to avoid in the hours before bed

This section often makes a bigger difference faster than the "what to eat" section. If you can only change one thing, start here.

  • Coffee, strong tea, and energy drinks after 3pm. Caffeine stays in your system longer than most people think — about half of what you drink at 3pm is still in your body at 8–9pm. Sensitive people should stop even earlier. If you rely on an afternoon drink to stay alert, What to Drink Instead of Coffee has a few alternatives.
  • Alcohol. Alcohol makes many people fall asleep quickly but disrupts the structure of sleep in the second half of the night, making you more likely to wake up between 2–4am and still feel tired in the morning.
  • Late dinners heavy in oil, fried foods, or hot pot and grilled dishes. Fat makes the stomach work longer. Lying down right after eating is a common recipe for heartburn and bloating.
  • Spicy foods. For many people, spicy dishes close to bedtime cause a burning sensation in the chest and slightly raise body temperature — the opposite of what the body needs when preparing for sleep.
  • Rich sweets, candy, and evening bubble tea. Besides the rise and fall in blood sugar, bubble tea also carries caffeine that many people forget about.
  • Drinking too much water right before bed. Staying hydrated throughout the day matters more than drinking a lot in the evening; loading up on water at 10pm often leads to a bathroom trip at 2am. See more in How to Drink Water the Right Way Every Day.
For most people, cutting out the afternoon coffee and moving dinner an hour earlier makes a bigger difference than any single "sleep-friendly" food.

How long before bed should you eat

This question has two layers, and mixing them together is why many people find sleep advice confusing.

For your main dinner: the most comfortable gap is 3 hours before bed. If you sleep at 11pm, dinner should be finished by 8pm. People who often experience reflux usually need a wider gap, 3–4 hours, and should avoid lying down right after eating.

For a small snack: 60–90 minutes is enough. A banana or a cup of warm nut milk doesn't require the stomach to work as long as a full meal does.

A few common real-life situations:

  • You get home late from work, around 8:30pm. Split it into two: have a light snack in the late afternoon at work (nuts, yogurt, banana), then at home, keep dinner light — soup, vegetables, a bit of protein — instead of a full, heavy meal at 9pm.
  • You work out in the evening, finishing around 9pm. Your body needs protein to recover, but you don't want a heavy meal. A cup of nut milk or a serving of plant-based protein mixed with warm water is a lighter, easier-to-digest option than a late dinner. See more on the best time of day to have plant-based protein.
  • You're used to eating late at night and can't stop right away. Don't cut it off abruptly. Move the timing 20 minutes earlier each week and gradually lighten what you eat.

Portions: how much is enough for an evening snack

This is where many people go off track. A "proper" evening snack is usually only about 150–250 kcal — much smaller than what our hands tend to grab when we're hungry.

A few easy reference points, no need to weigh anything:

  • One medium banana: about 100 kcal.
  • 15 almonds: about 100 kcal.
  • A 100g cup of unsweetened yogurt: about 60–80 kcal.
  • A 200ml cup of unsweetened nut milk: about 60–90 kcal.
  • Half a boiled sweet potato: about 90 kcal.
  • A slice of whole-grain bread with a thin spread of peanut butter: about 160 kcal.

The simplest rule: pick one main item plus at most one side item, and put it on a small plate or bowl instead of eating straight from the bag. Eating straight from a large bag is the fastest way for a small handful of nuts to turn into half the bag.

One more small tip: ask yourself, "Am I actually hungry, or just bored?" Many evening cravings are habit-driven reflexes (sitting on the couch, turning on the screen, hands automatically reaching for food) rather than a genuine hunger signal. Drinking a cup of warm water and waiting 10 minutes is usually enough to tell the two apart.

A traditional Eastern medicine view: evenings should be warm, light, and early

Traditional Eastern medicine offers a very simple piece of advice about dinner: eat early, eat little, eat warm. This isn't a medical diagnosis — it's a way of observing and caring for yourself appropriately.

In this view, the digestive system is imagined as a fire that needs steady warmth. In the evening, as the body shifts into rest mode, that "fire" also cools down. Loading it up at that point with an oily, cold, or raw meal is like asking the fire to work hard just when it wants to rest — the result is a heavy, sluggish feeling, often described colloquially as "food sitting undigested."

Applying this in your own kitchen is quite simple:

  • Favor warm, soft foods in the evening: soup, thin porridge, boiled vegetables, steamed tofu — instead of cold salads, ice water, or ice cream.
  • Reduce oil in your last meal of the day, saving any fried food for lunch instead.
  • Eat slowly and chew thoroughly. A dinner eaten over 25 minutes feels very different from one wolfed down in 8 minutes, even if the amount of food is the same. Eating Slowly and Chewing Thoroughly covers this in more depth.

Everyone tends to have a different natural constitution: people who often feel a cold stomach or cold hands and feet tend to feel noticeably better with warm foods; people who tend to run hot may prefer cooler, lighter foods, as long as they avoid anything too cold right before bed. That's why IKI doesn't offer a single meal plan for everyone, and instead starts by understanding each person's constitution — you can learn more in The 5 Constitution Types in Traditional Eastern Medicine.

Five easy evening snack combos in 5 minutes

A list of ingredients is easy to remember, but at 9:30pm when you're hungry, what you actually need is a specific combo. The five suggestions below can all be made in about 5 minutes.

  1. The classic warming combo: 3 tablespoons of quick-cook rolled oats with 150ml boiling water, plus half a sliced banana and a small handful of pumpkin seeds. Eat warm, no added sugar.
  2. The combo for evening workouts: a serving of plant-based protein mixed with 250ml warm water, sipped slowly, with half a banana. Light on the stomach while still providing enough protein for recovery.
  3. The laziest combo: a cup of unsweetened yogurt left out to lose its chill + 5 almonds broken in half. No cooking, no dishes to wash.
  4. The Vietnamese-style combo: a bowl of hot ginger tofu pudding with little sugar. Warming and provides soy protein.
  5. The sweet-and-gentle combo: half a warm boiled sweet potato + a cup of caffeine-free herbal tea. Enough to satisfy a sweet craving without opening a bag of snacks.

Diet is only half — your evening routine is the other half

If you've gotten the eating part right but are still tossing and turning, the sticking point is almost certainly in the group of habits below.

  • Light. Strong white light in the evening signals "it's still daytime" to your body clock. Dim the overhead lights and use warm yellow lighting in your living space after 9pm.
  • Screens. It's not just about blue light, but content — scrolling through news or social media keeps the mind in a stimulated state. Screens Before Bedtime has suggestions for cutting back gradually.
  • A consistent bedtime. The body likes predictability. Going to bed and waking up within the same 30-minute window, even on weekends, is one of the most effective and completely free things you can do.
  • Room temperature and a small ritual. A slightly cool room, a warm shower 60–90 minutes before bed, or soaking your feet in warm water — these are all familiar signals that help your body shift state.
  • Unprocessed stress. If your mind keeps replaying an unfinished to-do list, write it down before getting into bed. Putting it on paper is a simple way to let your brain let go.

A few special cases that need their own adjustments

People with frequent reflux or heartburn. Prioritize a 3–4 hour gap between dinner and bedtime, avoid lying down right after eating, and limit sour foods, tomatoes, mint, chocolate, and fried food at dinner. Slightly raising the head of the bed also helps many people feel more comfortable. See more in the article on acid reflux.

Night-shift workers. The same principles apply, just at a shifted schedule: your "dinner" is the meal before your main sleep period, even if that period falls at 8am. Keep it light and warm, and keep your sleep time consistent even on days off. Health for Night-Shift Workers goes deeper into this.

Older adults. Both hunger and thirst signals tend to decrease with age, so dinner can easily end up too small, leading to waking up hungry. A small, warm, gently protein-rich snack like nut milk or thin porridge often works well.

People with existing health conditions or on prescribed medication. Especially for blood sugar, kidney, or stomach issues — check with your doctor before changing your evening eating habits, especially if you're planning to add any dietary supplement.

When to see a doctor. If difficulty sleeping persists for more than a month, along with loud snoring and pauses in breathing during sleep, severe daytime fatigue, or noticeable mood changes — that's the time to see a doctor rather than continuing to self-adjust through diet.

A 7-day trial to find the evening routine that suits you

You don't need to change everything at once. Try this pace and jot down a short note each morning about how you felt.

  • Days 1–2: keep everything else the same, just move dinner 30–60 minutes earlier. Note: how long did it take to fall asleep last night?
  • Day 3: cut out coffee, strong tea, and bubble tea entirely after 3pm.
  • Day 4: add a small, warm snack 60–90 minutes before bed — pick one of the combos above.
  • Day 5: dim the lights and keep your phone away from bed, replacing it with 10 minutes of reading a physical book.
  • Day 6: add a warming ritual — a warm shower or foot soak before bed.
  • Day 7: stick to a fixed bedtime even if you're not sleepy yet; lie down and let your body get used to that schedule.

After a week, look back at your seven notes. You'll see fairly clearly which change made a difference for you specifically — and that's what's worth keeping, not a generic list from anyone else.

In closing: a small snack, a consistent evening routine

The short answer to what to eat before bed for better sleep: choose something small, warm, low in oil and low in sugar — banana, nuts, oats, yogurt, warm nut milk, sweet potato, tofu — eaten 60–90 minutes before bed. At the same time, move your main dinner earlier and cut caffeine from early afternoon onward.

But perhaps the most important thing to remember is: sleep isn't something you can buy with a single meal. It's the cumulative result of an entire day — how you ate, how you moved, when you were exposed to light, and whether you gave yourself a quiet moment before lying down. Your evening snack is just one link in that chain, but it's the easiest one to adjust as a starting point.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I eat before bed for better sleep?

Choose a small, warm snack that's low in oil and low in sugar: a banana, 10–15 almonds, a bowl of thin oatmeal porridge, unsweetened yogurt, kiwi, a cup of warm nut milk, or half a boiled sweet potato. Finish eating about 60–90 minutes before getting into bed. One or two items are enough — no need to eat several things at once.

How long before bed should I eat dinner?

For your main dinner, the most comfortable gap is 3 hours before bed; people who often experience reflux should allow 3–4 hours and avoid lying down right after eating. For a small snack like a banana or a cup of warm nut milk, 60–90 minutes is enough.

If I feel hungry at bedtime, should I skip eating or have a snack?

Try not to force yourself to go hungry when your stomach is growling, since hunger can easily wake you up in the middle of the night. The sensible approach is a very small snack, around 150–250 kcal, choosing something warm and easy to digest. If you're not sure whether you're truly hungry or just bored, drink a cup of warm water and wait 10 minutes.

Does drinking milk before bed help you sleep better?

Milk and nut milk provide tryptophan along with a bit of carbohydrate, and a warm cup can feel comforting as a ritual to close out the day. However, this is only a small factor; consistent timing, evening light exposure, and stress levels still play the biggest role in sleep quality. People who are lactose intolerant should choose nut milk instead.

What foods should I avoid in the evening?

It's best to avoid coffee, strong tea, energy drinks, and bubble tea after 3pm; alcohol; late dinners heavy in oil, fried foods, or hot pot and grilled dishes; spicy foods; rich sweets and candy; and drinking too much water right before bed.

What should I eat before bed after a late evening workout?

Your body needs protein to recover, but you shouldn't eat a heavy meal right before bed. A cup of warm nut milk, a serving of diluted plant-based protein, or a boiled egg with half a banana are neat, easy-to-digest choices compared to a late meal.
