Is Eating Vegetarian All of Lunar July Nutritionally Enough? How to Stay Healthy Through the Vu Lan Season

IKI Health Coach Team· Updated 2026-08-12· 15 min read
Quick Answer

Eating vegetarian for all of Lunar July can be fully nutritious if you replace rather than simply remove. The four most common gaps are protein, iron, zinc, and vitamin B12. As long as each meal has one clear protein source (tofu, beans, mushrooms, nuts), you pair it with vitamin C for better iron absorption, and you avoid an all-starch, deep-fried style of eating, you'll get through the vegetarian month feeling light and steady.

When Lunar July arrives, many Vietnamese families switch to vegetarian eating — some only on the full moon and new moon days, others for the entire month. And the question we hear most often during these weeks is: is eating vegetarian all of Lunar July nutritionally enough?

The honest answer: yes, if you replace; no, if you only remove.

This is the line between a vegetarian month that leaves you feeling light and clear-headed, and one that leaves you sluggish, constantly hungry, and counting down the days by the third week. The difference isn't whether you eat meat or not — it's what you replace the meat and fish with.

This article will point out the four most common gaps that appear during a full month of vegetarian eating, how to fill each one with foods you can find at any Vietnamese market, a sample seven-day menu, and notes for specific groups of people. The goal is to help you get through the Vu Lan season whole in both spirit and health.

Why many people feel tired eating vegetarian in July

Take a look at a typical vegetarian plate served in many households this month: white rice, stir-fried water spinach with garlic, fried tofu, fried vegetarian spring rolls, vegetable soup. It looks complete, but break it down and you'll see the issue:

  • The main protein source is fried tofu — and much of its weight is actually absorbed oil
  • Vegetarian spring rolls and mock meats are mostly starch and oil, contributing very little protein
  • Stir-fried vegetables and soup provide micronutrients but almost no protein
  • Overall: a meal high in starch and oil, with a clear protein shortfall

Eating this way for a few days is fine. But keep it up for three or four weeks straight, and the body speaks up in familiar ways: hunger returns about two hours after eating, sugar cravings hit in the afternoon, energy dips mid-day, hair and nails feel weaker, and workouts feel harder to push through.

People then conclude, "vegetarian eating just doesn't suit me." The real conclusion should be: that plate wasn't built to be nutritionally complete.

There are three common pitfalls:

Pitfall one — an all-starch vegetarian diet. Meat and fish are removed with nothing added in their place, and the empty space is filled with rice, noodles, or bread. You feel full, but protein is missing.

Pitfall two — relying on packaged vegetarian mock meats. Vegetarian ham, sausages, and packaged mock meats are convenient and mimic meat dishes, but many are high in salt and oil while offering little protein. They should be an occasional item, not the foundation of your meals.

Pitfall three — frying everything. This is a natural instinct: vegetarian dishes can taste bland, so frying adds flavor. But when tofu, mushrooms, and vegetables are all fried, the plate becomes an oil-heavy one.

Everyone's body is different — some people feel lighter eating vegetarian, while others feel drained by the very first week. Four gaps to fill during a month of vegetarian eating

When you remove meat, fish, and eggs, four nutrient groups drop the most noticeably. These are the four you need to actively replace.

Gap 1: Protein

This is the biggest gap and also the easiest to fill. Plant foods offer plenty of protein sources — you just need to name them and include them in every meal.

| Plant Protein Source | Suggested Serving | Estimated Protein | |---|---|---| | Firm pressed tofu | 100g | about 12–16g | | Soft tofu | 100g | about 6–8g | | Boiled soybeans (edamame) | 100g beans | about 11g | | Cooked lentils | 1 small bowl | about 9g | | Cooked black beans, red beans | 1 small bowl | about 8g | | Mushrooms (various) | 100g | about 3g | | Pumpkin seeds, sunflower seeds | 1 handful (30g) | about 6–9g | | Almonds, walnuts | 1 handful (30g) | about 5–6g | | Soy milk | 1 glass (250ml) | about 7g | | Cooked brown rice | 1 bowl | about 5g |

These figures are reference estimates and vary by type and preparation method.

The simplest practical rule: every main meal should have one clearly visible protein source on the plate. No need to count grams. Just look at your plate and answer, "which dish is the protein of this meal?" If the answer is "none" or "probably in the vegetables," that meal needs more.

For people who exercise regularly or those over 50 — two groups with higher protein needs — a month of vegetarian eating can be a challenging stretch. Many people add a glass of plant-based protein as a snack to make sure their total daily protein doesn't drop, especially on busy days when there isn't time to prepare a fully balanced vegetarian meal.

Gap 2: Iron

Plant-based iron is absorbed less efficiently by the body than iron from red meat. This doesn't mean you'll necessarily fall short — it just means you need a few tricks to boost absorption.

Common plant iron sources: lentils, black beans, chickpeas, amaranth leaves, malabar spinach, spinach, black sesame, pumpkin seeds, tofu, and whole grains.

Two tips to boost absorption:

Pair with vitamin C. Vitamin C significantly boosts absorption of plant-based iron. This is easy to do in Vietnamese meals: squeeze lime into soup, eat guava or orange after a meal, add tomato to braised tofu, or eat plenty of fresh herbs with raw vegetables.

Avoid strong tea and coffee right after meals. Tannins in strong tea interfere with iron absorption. Space it out by at least an hour after eating. If you're used to having tea after rice, switching to a cup of gentle herbal tea a bit later works both ways — you keep the relaxing after-meal ritual while not interfering with the iron you just consumed.

Gap 3: Zinc

Zinc faces a similar absorption issue as iron, because phytates in grains and legumes bind with it and reduce absorption.

Plant zinc sources: pumpkin seeds (very rich), sesame seeds, chickpeas, lentils, cashews, mushrooms, whole grains, and bean sprouts.

A useful trick: soaking and sprouting. Soak beans and seeds for a few hours before cooking, then discard the soaking water — this reduces phytates and improves absorption. This is also why traditional fermented foods like soybean paste, fermented tofu, and miso hold value — the fermentation process naturally lowers phytate content.

A daily handful of roasted pumpkin seeds is the simplest and most effective way to fill the zinc gap during your vegetarian month.

Gap 4: Vitamin B12

B12 is almost entirely absent from plant foods. This point needs to be stated clearly.

Good news for a month-long fast: the body stores B12 in the liver in amounts that last quite a while, so one month of vegetarian eating rarely causes problems for someone who normally eats a mixed diet.

However, if you eat vegetarian year-round, or for many consecutive months, this is the nutrient that needs the most attention. Sources include B12-fortified foods (some plant milks, breakfast cereals, nutritional yeast labeled as fortified) or supplements. Talk to a doctor or nutritionist for the right amount rather than guessing on your own.

How to build a nutritionally complete vegetarian plate

There's an easy formula to check whether your vegetarian plate is complete. Divide your plate into four parts:

One quarter — protein source. Tofu (braised, steamed, stir-fried — not necessarily fried), stewed beans, meaty mushrooms like king oyster and shiitake, tempeh, fermented tofu. This is the part most often missing.

One quarter — starch. Prioritize brown rice, sweet potato, taro, or brown rice noodles over plain white rice. Whole-grain starches digest more slowly and keep you full longer.

Half — a variety of colorful vegetables. The more colors, the more varied the micronutrients: dark green (malabar spinach, mustard greens, amaranth), orange-red (carrots, pumpkin, tomatoes), white (radish, kohlrabi), purple (purple cabbage, eggplant).

Plus a bit of healthy fat: a spoonful of olive oil or sesame oil drizzled over the dish, a handful of nuts sprinkled on top, a few slices of avocado.

And a touch of vitamin C at the end of the meal: a piece of guava, an orange segment, or a squeeze of lime in your soup.

The three changes that matter most

If you can only do three things during your vegetarian month, choose these: (1) have a clearly visible protein source at every meal; (2) swap part of your white rice for brown rice or sweet potato; (3) limit fried dishes to no more than one per meal. These three changes address most of the reasons people feel tired during extended vegetarian eating.

Sample seven-day menu

This is a suggestion to help you visualize your meals, not a rigid template. Feel free to adjust based on the season and what's available at your local market.

Day 1

  • Breakfast: oatmeal cooked with soy milk, topped with banana and a spoonful of chia seeds
  • Lunch: brown rice, tofu braised with shiitake mushrooms, boiled water spinach, pumpkin soup, guava for dessert
  • Dinner: stir-fried rice noodles with mushrooms and vegetables, soft tofu soup with tomato

Day 2

  • Breakfast: whole-grain bread with peanut butter and banana, soy milk
  • Lunch: rice, lentils stewed with carrot and potato, boiled malabar spinach, orange for dessert
  • Dinner: mung bean and mushroom porridge, mixed vegetable salad with sesame oil and roasted pumpkin seeds

Day 3

  • Breakfast: black bean sticky rice, plant milk
  • Lunch: brown rice, steamed tofu patty (not fried), garlic-stir-fried mustard greens, sour amaranth soup
  • Dinner: rice noodle soup with straw mushrooms and soft tofu, plus fresh herbs

Day 4

  • Breakfast: banana smoothie with soy milk, avocado, and oats
  • Lunch: rice, braised chickpeas, boiled zucchini, mushroom and seaweed soup, pineapple for dessert
  • Dinner: steamed sweet potato, pumpkin soup with lotus seeds, cucumber and tomato salad

Day 5

  • Breakfast: vegetarian "bun rieu" (tofu, tomato, fermented rice vinegar broth)
  • Lunch: brown rice, steamed tofu skin rolls with mushrooms, boiled sweet potato leaves, sour vegetarian soup, guava for dessert
  • Dinner: brown rice and red bean porridge, a handful of mixed nuts

Day 6

  • Breakfast: vegetarian steamed rice rolls or rice dumplings, soy milk
  • Lunch: rice, tofu in tomato sauce, boiled mustard greens, mushroom and malabar spinach soup, orange
  • Dinner: vegetarian pho with mushroom and vegetable broth, plus soft tofu

Day 7

  • Breakfast: mung bean porridge, a banana
  • Lunch: brown rice, king oyster mushrooms braised with pepper, stir-fried chayote, tofu and chive soup, papaya for dessert
  • Dinner: a light meal — sweet potato, plant milk, a handful of nuts

You'll notice what all seven days have in common: every main meal has a clear protein source, and most dishes are braised, steamed, boiled, or simmered rather than fried. That's the whole secret.

Notes for specific groups

Busy working people. The biggest obstacle isn't knowledge — it's time. Prepare ahead over the weekend: soak and boil a batch of beans to keep in the fridge, roast a jar of pumpkin and sunflower seeds, and pre-wash vegetables. When eating out for lunch, choose a vegetarian restaurant with braised or steamed dishes rather than one serving only fried food.

People who exercise. Higher protein needs mean the vegetarian month requires more careful planning. Spread protein across several small meals, add a protein-rich snack, and don't cut your total calories — many people unintentionally eat less overall during their vegetarian month and feel drained, when the real cause is insufficient energy, not just protein.

People over 50. The body's ability to build muscle protein gradually declines with age, so during an extended vegetarian period, it's even more important to maintain adequate protein. Prioritize tofu, soybeans, and lentils — the plant sources with the best protein quality.

People with underlying health conditions or on medication. Talk to your doctor first. Some dietary changes can affect how medications work, especially for people with kidney conditions or those on blood thinners.

Pregnant women, breastfeeding mothers, and growing children. These groups have specific nutritional needs. Vegetarian eating can still work, but meal planning should be guided by a nutritionist or doctor rather than done on your own.

People recovering from illness. If you've just recovered from an illness and are still in the recovery phase, suddenly switching to a full month of vegetarian eating may not be the best choice. Consider partial vegetarian eating (full moon and new moon days, or vegetarian dinners only) instead of the whole month.

Flexible vegetarian eating — an option few mention

One final point worth making: you don't have to choose between "vegetarian all month" and "not vegetarian at all."

Many families choose a middle path that fits better with their actual daily rhythm:

  • Fully vegetarian on the full moon, new moon, and a few fixed days each month
  • Vegetarian dinners every day, eating normally at other meals
  • Vegetarian for the first two weeks of the month, normal eating for the rest
  • Vegetarian at home, flexible when eating out or traveling for work

Any of these approaches holds value both spiritually and health-wise, as long as you stay consistent and don't put pressure on yourself. A whole month of half-hearted vegetarian eating just to complete the days isn't better than two weeks of vegetarian eating done well and nutritionally complete.

The most important thing when Lunar July ends: don't immediately swing back to unrestrained eating. Your body has just gotten used to a lighter diet with more vegetables. Try to hold on to a few good habits you've built — more vegetables, less fried food, tofu and beans in your weekly rotation. That's the lasting value of the vegetarian month.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will eating vegetarian for a full month cause nutrient deficiencies?

A month isn't a very long stretch, so if you eat a varied diet, your body can manage it well. The real risk isn't removing meat and fish — it's what you replace them with. A vegetarian plate made up only of white rice, stir-fried vegetables, and fried food is the kind of eating that tends to leave people feeling sluggish, not because it's vegetarian, but because it's low in protein and micronutrients.

Why do I feel hungry quickly and crave sweets after eating vegetarian meals?

This usually happens when a meal lacks protein and healthy fats, and is mostly starch. Starch digests quickly, blood sugar rises then falls, and hunger returns sooner. The fix is to add a clear protein source to every meal — tofu, beans, or mushrooms — along with a bit of nuts or plant oil. You'll stay full much longer without needing to eat more rice.

Which nutrients should vegetarians pay the most attention to in July?

The four to keep in mind are protein, iron, zinc, and vitamin B12. Over just one month, B12 rarely causes problems since the body has stored reserves, but if you eat vegetarian year-round, it becomes the nutrient to watch most closely. Plant-based iron and zinc are absorbed less efficiently than from animal sources, so pair meals with vitamin C-rich foods and avoid strong tea right after eating.

Should I eat a lot of mock-meat vegetarian products?

Treat them as an occasional item rather than the foundation of your meals. Many packaged vegetarian mock-meat products are high in salt and oil, and are often fried during preparation. If your vegetarian plate is mostly mock ham, mock sausage, and fried food every day, the month won't feel as light as you'd hoped. Let tofu, beans, mushrooms, and real vegetables form the base instead.

Who should be cautious about eating vegetarian for a full month?

Pregnant and breastfeeding women, growing children, older adults, people recovering from illness, and those with underlying health conditions or on long-term medication should talk to a doctor or nutritionist before committing to a full month of continuous vegetarian eating. It's not that vegetarian eating is harmful — it's that these groups have specific nutritional needs that require more careful planning.
