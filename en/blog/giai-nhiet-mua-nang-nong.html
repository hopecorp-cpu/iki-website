Staying Cool in Hot Weather the Right Way: Hydration, Cooling Foods, and a Summer Rhythm

IKI Health Coach Team· Updated 2026-07-24· 14 min read
Quick answer

To feel more comfortable on hot days, rehydrate steadily throughout the day (around 2–2.5 liters, or more when you sweat heavily), favoring room-temperature or mildly cool water over lots of iced water. Eat light, with plenty of vegetables, soups, and fruit traditionally considered cool-natured; limit deep-fried, spicy food and sugary drinks. Choose gentle herbal drinks instead of iced milk tea, avoid sudden hot-cold transitions, and schedule heavy tasks for the cooler parts of the day. If you feel intense dizziness, confusion, or faint, those are dangerous signs that call for emergency care right away.

This article shares proactive wellness knowledge and does not replace medical diagnosis or advice. On a scorching day, if you or someone near you shows signs of intense dizziness, confusion, incoherent speech, fainting, seizures, or hot red skin without sweating, these may indicate heatstroke or heat shock — move them to a cool place, cool the body, and call emergency services right away.

Every peak of summer, the sweltering heat throws our daily rhythm off balance: sleep is shallow, food loses its appeal, the body feels perpetually sluggish, and all you want is to reach for a glass of ice-cold water to "cool down." But the way we react to the heat sometimes leaves the body even more tired — gulping down iced water right after coming in from the sun, sitting for hours in a deeply air-conditioned room and then stepping straight outdoors, or eating erratically because nothing tastes good. This article shares how to cool off in hot weather gently and sustainably: rehydrating on a good rhythm, eating light and seasonal, choosing gentler drinks, avoiding heat shock, and rearranging your routine so the whole day feels easier to get through.

Why hot weather leads to dehydration and fatigue

When it's hot, the body works harder to keep its temperature stable. The main mechanism is sweating: as sweat evaporates, it carries heat away and cools you down. But along with that, water and mineral salts are lost continuously — often so quietly that you don't realize you've fallen short on water.

When your body's water level drops, the first signs are often easy to overlook or misread: fatigue, sluggishness, a mild headache, trouble concentrating, mild calf cramps, or a queasy, unsettled feeling. Many people assume they're just "overheated inside" or drowsy mid-morning, when in fact the body is asking to be rehydrated.

Hot weather also brings a few other changes that can leave us more tired:

  • Shallower sleep: high nighttime temperatures make sleep fitful, so you wake up still feeling heavy. This is a good time to look after your quality of sleep so the body has time to recover.
  • Poor appetite, careless eating: heat dulls the appetite, easily leading to erratic, nutrient-poor meals.
  • Overdoing cold and sugary things: iced water, milk tea, and ice cream refresh you instantly but usually carry a lot of sugar, making the energy dip that follows all the more noticeable.

Understanding how hard the body is "straining" in the heat helps us support it more gently, instead of just chasing an instant feeling of coolness.

Rehydrating the right way on hot days

Rehydrating is the single most important thing in hot weather, but "drinking a lot" isn't the same as "drinking well." A few simple principles help keep your body in comfortable balance:

  • Sip steadily throughout the day: take small, regular sips instead of waiting until your throat is parched and then gulping it all at once. Thirst usually shows up only once the body is already a little short on water.
  • Increase your intake with how much you sweat: on scorching days or when you're moving and active outdoors, your need can reach 2.5 liters or more. Proactively drink extra around active periods.
  • Favor mildly cool water over very cold ice water: room-temperature or mildly cool water quenches thirst while being gentle on the stomach, especially right after coming in from the sun.
  • Replace minerals when you sweat heavily: for heavy labor or intense exercise outdoors, when you sweat a lot, alongside plain water you can add water with a little mineral salt, coconut water, or a light broth to replace the minerals lost.

A very simple self-check your body gives you for free is the color of your urine: light yellow signals good balance, while dark yellow and scant urine reminds you to drink more. This is far gentler than trying to count exact liters each day.

In hot weather, drinking steadily and early matters more than drinking a lot at once. Don't wait until you're thirsty to drink, and don't wait until you're exhausted to rest.

A yin-yang view: cool-natured summer foods

In folk culinary experience and Eastern Traditional Medicine, food is often viewed through the balance between yin (cool) and yang (warm, hot). This isn't a way to diagnose illness, but a way of observing that helps us eat in tune with the season and with our own constitution.

People in the past believed summer belongs to yang and the weather is hot, so a sensible taste and style of cooking leans toward dishes considered cool-natured, light, and full of water — to balance out the heat outside. Conversely, eating too much spicy, deep-fried, or charred food on a hot day tends to leave people feeling stuffy and uncomfortable.

Interestingly, this folk view is quite close to the body's natural instinct: in the midday heat, few people crave a bowl of steaming spicy hotpot, and most feel more comfortable with a cool bowl of vegetable soup, a plate of cucumber, or a slice of watermelon. The yin-yang tradition here acts like a gentle culinary compass, hinting at which direction to lean by season, rather than a rigid rule.

Foods and dishes that help the body feel more comfortable

You don't need to overhaul your whole diet — just tilt the balance gently toward the light and cooling on sweltering days. A few familiar, easy-to-apply ideas:

Water-rich vegetables and produce

  • Leafy greens, Malabar spinach, jute leaves, water spinach: make a light, cooling soup that's easy to eat on a hot day. You can also see more suggestions in Leafy Greens Every Day.
  • Luffa, winter melon, bottle gourd, cucumber: full of water and light in flavor, ideal to boil or make into a gentle soup.
  • Tomato, bean sprouts, and beans: easy to work into everyday meals, helping make the meal feel lighter.

Juicy fruit

  • Watermelon, dragon fruit, orange, pomelo, pear: they rehydrate while providing vitamins, a refreshing dessert in place of ice cream or sugary sweet soups.
  • Eat whole pieces rather than juicing: this keeps the fiber and fills you up longer, avoiding taking in too much sugar at once.

Cooking methods to favor

  • Lean toward boiling, steaming, and simmering soups rather than deep-frying in oil or charring.
  • Cut back on food that's too spicy or too salty — both to feel less stuffy and to avoid a rush of thirst after the meal.
  • Split meals if your appetite is poor: eating a little but regularly is better than skipping a meal and then overeating heavily.

Gentle herbal drinks for hot days

In hot weather, the familiar reflex for many people is to reach for an iced milk tea, a fizzy soda, or an iced milk coffee to "wake up." The problem is that these drinks usually carry a significant amount of sugar, leaving you refreshed for a moment and then heavy and sluggish afterward. A gentler direction is to swap some of these for mild herbal drinks with little or no sugar.

A few options that are easy to prepare at home:

  • Plain water with a few slices of lemon, cucumber, or mint: a touch of fresh flavor makes it easier to drink than plain water, and it's handy to carry with you all day.
  • Herbal tea cooled down, enjoyed mildly cool: steep the tea, let it cool a little, and enjoy it at room temperature or mildly cool in place of iced sugary drinks.
  • Fresh coconut water: a natural, water-rich option suited to times after heavy sweating — use in moderation, don't overdo it.

Avoiding heat shock from sudden hot-cold transitions

One of the most common summer mistakes is letting the body change temperature too fast: walking under scorching sun and then stepping straight into a deeply air-conditioned room, or sitting a long time in a cold room and then heading right out into the midday heat. This sudden difference forces the body to adjust abruptly and can easily cause dizziness and fatigue.

A few small habits make the transition gentler:

  • Rest in an in-between area: when you come in from the sun, stand and rest for a few minutes somewhere airy, wipe off some sweat, and let your body cool naturally before entering a deeply air-conditioned room.
  • Keep the air conditioner at a moderate level: around 26–27°C, avoiding too large a gap with the outdoor temperature. Setting it very low won't make you healthier and can leave your throat dry and your body tired.
  • Don't shower in very cold water right after coming in from the sun: let the sweat dry off and the body settle before showering with moderately cool water.
  • Shield yourself during peak sun: a hat, sun-protective clothing, sunglasses, and shade all help reduce the direct impact of harsh sunlight.

The most important thing is to listen to your body's warning signs. Mild heat exhaustion may show up as feeling faint, a headache, heavy thirst, or hot skin — at which point you should get to a cool spot, rest, and rehydrate right away. But if severe signs appear as mentioned at the top of the article (intense dizziness, confusion, fainting, seizures), don't hesitate — call emergency services.

A summer rhythm: rest and movement

Cooling off isn't only about what you eat and drink — it's also about how you arrange your day. Living in tune with the rhythm of the hot season helps the body strain less.

A more comfortable summer day
  • 1
    Early morning — make the most of the cooler hours
    Drink a glass of water after waking, eat a light breakfast, and save the tasks that need physical effort for the cool early hours of the day.
  • 2
    Midday heat — limit exposure to the sun
    If you can, avoid heavy work or lengthy travel under the peak sun (roughly 11am–3pm). A short midday rest helps the body recover.
  • 3
    Late afternoon — light movement as it cools
    Walking or moving at a moderate pace in the late afternoon is far more comfortable than at midday. Remember to bring water and sip steadily.
  • 4
    Evening — prepare for a cool night's sleep
    Shower with moderately cool water, keep the room airy, and limit screens close to bedtime so you fall asleep more easily even in the heat.

Exercise is still good in hot weather; you just need to pick the right time and know when to stop when the body signals. If you train outdoors, favor early morning or late afternoon, rehydrate in small sips before, during, and after, and don't push too hard when you feel dizzy or your heart is beating unusually fast.

Common mistakes when trying to cool off

"The more ice, the cooler." Iced water gives an instant refreshing feeling, but drinking a lot of it on an empty stomach or right after coming in from the sun can easily upset the stomach. Mildly cool water is usually gentler.

"Just drink lots of soda and juice to rehydrate." Sugary sodas and juices can add energy you don't notice, while also making thirst return faster. Plain water should remain the foundation.

"The colder the air conditioner, the healthier." A temperature that's too low, with a big gap from outside, easily causes a dry throat, tiredness, and a higher risk of dizziness during sudden transitions. A moderate level is always more comfortable.

"If I've no appetite, just skip the meal to feel lighter." Skipping meals leaves the body short on energy and even more tired in the heat. If your appetite is poor, split your meals and choose light, easy-to-digest dishes.

"Just rest whenever I feel too tired." In scorching heat, waiting until you're exhausted to rest is sometimes already too late. Proactively get to a cool place and rehydrate early, as soon as you notice the first signs.

A suggested 7-day practice for hot weather

You don't need to change everything at once. Try a small rhythm over one week and keep whatever suits you:

  • Days 1–2: keep a water bottle within sight and sip steadily all day, watching the color of your urine to self-adjust your intake.
  • Days 3–4: add a light, cooling dish to a meal (vegetable soup, a plate of cucumber, a piece of juicy fruit) in place of a deep-fried dish or something sugary.
  • Day 5: try swapping your afternoon iced milk tea or soda for a mild, low-sugar herbal drink.
  • Day 6: set the air conditioner to a moderate level and build the habit of resting a few minutes somewhere airy before moving between hot and cold.
  • Day 7: shift the tasks that take physical effort to early morning or late afternoon, and notice whether the day feels less sluggish.

After a week, look back and see whether your midday feels less tiring, your sleep deeper, and whether you rely less on cold, sugary drinks. Small but consistent changes tend to last far longer than trying to "cool off in a hurry" and then slipping back into old habits.

In closing: live with the season, feel better every day

Staying cool in hot weather the right way isn't about finding a "miracle" drink or dish. It's the combination of simple things: rehydrate steadily and early, eat light and seasonal, choose gentle drinks over cold sugary ones, avoid heat shock, and rearrange your daily rhythm to fit the heat. When you listen to your body and adjust with the season, every summer day feels much easier to breathe through.

Frequently asked questions

How much water should I drink each day in hot weather?

A common reference for adults is roughly 1.5–2 liters a day, but on scorching days or when you sweat a lot, the need rises noticeably and can reach 2.5 liters or more. The key is to sip steadily throughout the day rather than waiting until you're very thirsty and gulping it down. A simple way to self-check is to watch the color of your urine: light yellow is fine, dark yellow usually signals it's time to drink more.

Should I drink a lot of iced water when it's hot?

A cool glass of water on a hot day is perfectly normal and pleasant. What's worth limiting is drinking a lot of iced water on an empty stomach, right after coming in from the sun, or when the body is tired, since very cold water can leave the stomach uncomfortable. Room-temperature or mildly cool water usually quenches thirst while being gentler on the body than very cold ice water.

What should I eat to feel cooler in summer?

In the folk yin-yang view, hot weather suits foods traditionally considered cool-natured, such as leafy greens, luffa, winter melon, cucumber, watermelon, beans, and light vegetable soups. Favor boiled, steamed, and lightly simmered dishes over deep-fried, greasy, or spicy food. This is a culinary tradition for feeling more comfortable through the seasons, not a method for treating illness.

Do herbal drinks cool the body down?

Herbal drinks are relaxing beverages you can enjoy in place of sugary sodas or milk tea on a hot day, giving you a pleasant, low-sugar option. They are food supplements, not medicine, and do not replace medicine used to treat illness, so a herbal drink shouldn't be seen as a way to cool the body or to solve a health problem. If you have an underlying condition or take medication, please consult a professional before using them regularly.

How do I avoid heat shock when moving between an air-conditioned room and the hot outdoors?

Limit sudden temperature changes: when you come in from the sun, rest for a few minutes to let your body cool before entering a deeply air-conditioned room; when leaving a cold room, pause for a while in an in-between area. Keep the air conditioner at a moderate level, around 26–27°C, avoid too large a gap with the outdoor temperature, and drink water steadily so your body adjusts more gently.

Which signs of heatstroke or heat shock need emergency care right away?

Seek emergency care immediately if severe signs appear, such as intense dizziness, confusion, incoherent speech, fainting, seizures, hot red skin without sweating, or heavy nausea or vomiting. These may indicate heat shock, a dangerous condition. While waiting for medical help, move the person to a cool place, loosen their clothing, and cool the body.
