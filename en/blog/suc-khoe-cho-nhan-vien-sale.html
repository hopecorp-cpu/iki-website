Health Care for Sales and Business Professionals

IKI Health Coach Team· Updated 2026-07-22· 15 min read
Quick answer

Sales and business professionals often travel a lot, eat irregularly, skip meals, entertain clients over drinks and carry ongoing sales pressure. Looking after your health in this line of work doesn't require upending your job — it's about putting a few small habits back in the right places during a client-chasing day: keeping grab-and-go food ready so you don't skip meals, drinking enough water, entertaining clients moderately and with limits, protecting your voice, sneaking in micro-movement between appointments, and prioritizing sleep so your mind is sharp when it's time to close. This is a journey of building lasting habits, not one week of white-knuckling it.

If you work in sales or business development, your days probably rarely look like the one before. In the morning you rush to your first client before you've had anything decent to eat; at noon you eat in the car or skip lunch entirely because a meeting ran long; in the afternoon you move nonstop between locations; and more than a few evenings you sit at a table entertaining clients over a few drinks to keep the relationship warm. Woven through all of it are the sales numbers always hanging over your head, back-to-back calls that leave your voice hoarse, and the feeling that you always have to be running at full power.

A salesperson's body wears down in a very particular way: not from heavy labor, but from an irregular rhythm of life and prolonged mental pressure. Because the work sweeps you along, taking care of yourself usually gets pushed to the bottom of the list — and by the time the body speaks up, things have already been building for a while. This article isn't asking you to quit your job or live as disciplined as an athlete. It simply helps you put a few small habits back in the right places within an already busy day, so you keep the stamina to go the distance in this career. That's the spirit of proactive wellness: small choices every day, adding up into a foundation.

How sales work affects the body

Before talking about solutions, let's name what's actually happening. Understanding the nature of the job helps you know what to adjust first. The descriptions below are just general observations about occupational habits, not a medical diagnosis.

Irregular eating and skipped meals. A client-chasing schedule rarely lines up with fixed meal times. Many people get used to skipping breakfast, having lunch at three in the afternoon, then eating very late and very large to make up for it. This binge-style eating makes energy swing up and down, and easily leaves you tired and heavy-bellied.

Constant eating out and fast food. For convenience, a salesperson's meals are often canteen rice, fast food and soft drinks to stay alert. Little vegetables, plenty of oil and sugar day after day gradually creates a sense of sluggishness.

Entertaining clients over drinks. In many business areas, the drinking table is almost part of the job. Drinking a lot and often is a real burden on the body, and this is a point that calls for a moderate approach with clear limits.

Talking a lot, going hoarse. Calling and consulting all day keeps the vocal cords working continuously, which easily leads to a dry throat and hoarseness, especially when you drink little water.

Lots of sitting and driving. Moving between locations means long stretches in a fixed posture, which easily leads to a sore back, tense neck and shoulders, and numb limbs.

Sales pressure and sleep. The end-of-month numbers, difficult clients and unmet targets create prolonged stress, which drags along poor sleep, short sleep, and a vicious cycle of fatigue.

What all of these have in common is that they can be improved with small adjustments — none of which demand that you quit or overhaul your whole workflow. We'll go through each one.

Eating while traveling and busy

The biggest reason salespeople eat irregularly is "no time and nothing decent ready to eat." Both are solvable with one principle: prepare in advance so that when you're busy, you just grab and eat.

The key is to keep a "grab-and-go stash" in your car, your bag or your desk drawer:

  • Nuts (almonds, walnuts, cashews, pumpkin seeds) portioned into small bags. They keep well, need no refrigeration, and a small handful is enough to hold off hunger between two appointments.
  • Bananas, apples, guava or seasonal fruit that's easy to carry, quick to eat, and needs no knife or board.
  • Unsweetened yogurt or a carton of unsweetened milk to stave off hunger while still getting some protein.
  • Whole-grain bread and boiled eggs prepared the night before, wrapped to take along for an early client-chasing breakfast.

A few easy-to-remember principles for eating in the middle of a busy day:

  1. Don't fast until night and then overeat. A small portion at the right time keeps energy steady and avoids an overly heavy dinner that disrupts sleep.
  2. Have protein at each eating occasion. Protein keeps you full longer and eases cravings for sweets and energy drinks mid-day. Eggs, yogurt, nuts and lean meat all work.
  3. Watch what you "drink without noticing." Milk coffee, energy drinks and soft drinks sipped to stay alert on the road can add up to a lot of energy each day. Favor plain water and unsweetened tea.
  4. When eating out, order with intention. Add a plate of vegetables, choose boiled or steamed dishes over deep-fried, order a right-sized portion of rice. Just a few small tweaks repeated daily make enough of a difference.

You don't need to be perfect. A missed meal doesn't ruin anything, just as one flawless meal doesn't save anything. What matters is the overall trend across the week. If your breakfast tends to fall through, keeping a good source of protein and starch in the morning is the easiest thing in the day to take charge of.

Entertaining clients over drinks, moderately

This is the most distinctive part of sales work, and also the part that calls for plain talk: the most sustainable way to protect your health is not to drink a great deal and then look for a way to undo it afterward, but to limit and be moderate from the start. There's no trick that turns heavy drinking harmless. So the focus here is how to keep your working relationships while still controlling how much goes into your body.

A few practical approaches:

  • Eat something before you sit down. Not being on an empty stomach makes it easier to stay in control and feel more comfortable. A light portion of rice or a little food beforehand helps.
  • Set a limit from the start. Decide on your maximum number of glasses before you sit down, and stick to it. A decision made ahead of time is always easier to keep than one made in the middle of the fun.
  • Alternate with plain water. Between each drink, have a glass of water. This slows your pace and helps you stay clear-headed.
  • Drink slowly, don't race. You don't have to keep up with the table's pace. Sip slowly and set your glass down between sips.
  • Practice a few polite ways to decline. "I'll just sip this one," or "I'm driving today" are ways to keep the mood without drinking beyond your limit. Many clients respect someone who knows moderation.
  • Don't drive after drinking. This is a safety line that shouldn't be negotiable. Book a ride or ask someone to take you home.

The right frame of mind is "reducing harm through moderation," treating each client dinner as something to manage rather than a contest. If your body shows unusual signs that persist, don't guess or self-treat — see a doctor for a proper assessment.

Protecting your voice and throat

For a salesperson, the voice is a working tool. Calling, consulting and presenting all day keeps the vocal cords running continuously, and when you drink little water, the throat gets drier and hoarseness comes more easily.

A few small habits help your voice last longer:

  • Drink water steadily all day. The vocal cords need to stay moist to work smoothly. Keep a bottle within reach and sip regularly — don't wait until you're thirsty.
  • Favor warm water. Warm water is gentler on the throat than water that's too cold, especially when you talk a lot.
  • Give your voice a rest between calls. A few minutes of quiet between back-to-back calls lets the vocal cords recover.
  • Don't force your voice when tired. Speaking loudly or straining once you're already hoarse tends to strain the throat further. Lower your volume and slow down.
  • Warm herbal tea is a pleasant option to sip mid-day — it keeps the throat moist and gives you a short pause.

If hoarseness lasts for several weeks without improving, or comes with soreness and discomfort, don't brush it off — see a specialist for a proper check.

Micro-movement between appointments

The biggest barrier for salespeople isn't laziness — it's the feeling that "you need a whole hour and a gym for it to count as exercise." Meanwhile, it's the constant sitting in cars and meetings that quietly makes your back sore and your neck and shoulders tense. The solution isn't one grand workout, but small movements spread evenly through the day.

Make use of the very gaps in your client-chasing schedule:

  • Stand up, stretch, roll your neck and shoulders each time you finish an appointment or step out of the car.
  • Take breaks on long drives. About every hour of driving, stop for a few minutes, get out of the car, walk around and stretch. It's both safer for driving and easier on the body.
  • Walk when you can. Park a little farther away, take the stairs for a few floors, walk briskly while waiting for a client.
  • Take phone calls while standing or walking instead of sitting still for the whole long call.
  • A few evening stretches right in your room, focused on the back, neck, shoulders and legs — the areas that bear the most from long sitting.

The key is to choose something so easy that it's hard to find an excuse to skip. Walking and stretching a little each day, kept up all year, means far more than one heavy workout followed by two weeks off. If you'd like a concrete list of gentle movement suited to people who sit and travel a lot, the article Light Daily Movement goes deeper into how to choose and keep it up without any equipment.

One important note: if you feel numbness in your hands or feet, or neck-and-shoulder pain radiating down your arm that won't go away, this isn't something to guess about. You can read the article Numbness in the Hands and Feet to understand more about this phenomenon, but if the symptoms are persistent or worsening, see a doctor for a proper assessment instead of self-treating.

Posture while driving and sitting in meetings

Sitting in one posture for a long time is hard to avoid in sales, but the way you sit makes a big difference to your back and neck.

  • Adjust the driver's seat so your back stays upright, not reclined so far that you have to lean forward. Keep your knees level with or slightly below your hips.
  • Keep your shoulders relaxed and don't crane your neck forward when looking at the road or your phone screen.
  • Put your phone on a mount at eye level when using maps, instead of hunching down to look — a prolonged head-down posture is a familiar cause of neck and shoulder strain.
  • When sitting in a meeting with a client, shift your posture slightly now and then, rather than holding one position the whole time.

These things sound very small, but repeated across thousands of hours of sitting in the car each year, the cumulative effect isn't small at all. Managing sales-target stress

Pressure over the numbers is a feature that comes with the job, and perhaps the biggest invisible burden. It affects not only your mood but also drags along insomnia, irregular eating and a feeling of exhaustion. The goal isn't to eliminate the pressure — that isn't realistic — but to keep it from taking over your whole day and your sleep.

A few ways to make the pressure easier to breathe under:

  • Break a big target into small steps. A whole month's number can feel overwhelming. Splitting it into weekly targets, daily targets, and then focusing on the task in front of you gives a clearer sense of control.
  • Separate work from rest time. Try not to open work messages once you're in bed. Set yourself an end-of-day marker to "close" work.
  • Have a screen-free way to unwind. A short walk, a chat with a loved one, some music, or just sitting quietly for a few minutes breathing slowly. A few small pauses each day give the mind room to settle.
  • Don't use alcohol as a regular way to relieve stress. It can create a temporary sense of relief but makes sleep fitful and leaves you more tired the next day.
  • Speak up when overwhelmed. Sharing with a colleague, manager or loved one sometimes lightens the burden more than you'd expect.

Prioritizing sleep for a sharp mind

For salespeople, alertness and the ability to adapt when meeting clients depend heavily on sleep. A poor night's sleep leaves you slow to react, easily irritated, less able to close, and reaching for coffee all day. Yet this job tends to involve early starts, late finishes and late-night client dinners, which erode sleeping hours.

If you can't sleep many hours, prioritize quality:

  1. Keep a fixed wake-up time, even after late client dinners. A steady wake-up time stabilizes your body clock faster than a fixed bedtime.
  2. Create a wind-down period before bed. Spend the last 20–30 minutes of the day not handling intense work, dimming the lights, doing something light like a warm bath.
  3. Be careful with late-afternoon coffee. For many people, one coffee after early afternoon is enough to wreck that night's sleep.
  4. Limit alcohol close to bedtime. Alcohol can make you drift off quickly but leaves sleep shallow and fitful, so you feel sluggish the next day.
  5. Keep your phone away from the bed. Scrolling work messages in bed both pushes bedtime back and winds your mind up more.

If you tend to toss and turn even after going to bed on time, the article Quality Sleep suggests ways to create a good sleep environment and calm the mind before bed. Enough sleep is a direct investment in the next day's work performance.

Drinking enough water all day

Salespeople easily fall into drinking very little plain water all day, replacing it with coffee, energy drinks and soft drinks. Being short on water makes you tired, less focused, dry-throated and hoarse, and prone to mistaking thirst for hunger.

The most sustainable way isn't to force yourself to drink a lot at once, but to make drinking water automatic:

  • Keep a bottle in the car and one on your desk, always within reach.
  • Tie drinking water to existing moments: a sip when you get in the car, a glass after each appointment, a glass when you get back to the office.
  • Start the morning with a glass of warm water before you touch coffee.

A simple way to self-check is the color of your urine: light yellow is fine, dark yellow is a sign to drink more. If you'd like to understand hydration suited to each person more deeply, the article Drink Water the Right Way Every Day, by Constitution goes into this topic in detail.

Common misconceptions

"If you're in sales, you have to accept wrecking your health — it's the price of the job." The job has its own nature, but most of the wear comes from habits rather than from the essence of the work. Eating with preparation, drinking moderately, sleeping enough and moving lightly are all things you can fit into a busy day without quitting.

"Drink a lot and then find a way to undo it — done." There's no way to make excessive drinking harmless. The real way to protect your body is to limit and be moderate from the start, set limits and keep to moderation.

"I'm too busy to take care of my health." Most of the habits in this article don't demand extra time — they just adjust how you use the time you already have: a small portion between two appointments, water while driving, a stretch when you step out of the car, turning off the phone a bit earlier.

"Closing deals matters more than enough sleep." Poor sleep leaves you slow to react and quick to lose your composure with difficult clients — which directly affects your ability to close. Sleep isn't the enemy of sales; it's the condition for sustainable sales.

"Food supplements will make up for an irregular lifestyle." Food supplements are only a support that goes alongside your lifestyle; they don't replace a balanced meal, sleep or moderate drinking. The foundation is always your daily habits.

In closing: keeping your strength for the long haul

Sales and business work demands that you always be in a state of readiness, and that's exactly what makes protecting your health more important, not less. You don't need to upend your job or give up the important client tables. You just need to put a few small habits back in the right places: a bag of grab-and-go food in the car so you don't skip meals, a clear limit when entertaining clients, a flask of warm water to protect your voice, a few minutes of stretching when you step out of the car, and sleep given a little more priority.

Choose the easiest thing to start right this week — usually preparing snacks in advance or drinking enough water — and do it until it becomes a natural reflex, then add the next one. Go slow and steady, adding one solid habit each month, and a year from now you'll find you've kept the form the job demands. The IKI app can be a companion that reminds you to keep these small habits amid a busy day, but what makes the difference is still your own steady choices, every day.

Frequently asked questions

What should a salesperson do when they keep skipping meals to chase clients?

Keep grab-and-go options ready in your car or bag: unsweetened yogurt, bananas, nuts, whole-grain bread, boiled eggs. When you have a short gap between two appointments, eating a small portion is better than fasting until night and then overeating. Piling everything into one big meal at the end of the day easily leads to a heavy stomach, poor sleep and fatigue the next day.

How can I protect my health if I regularly have to entertain clients over drinks?

The principle is to limit and be moderate, not to drink a lot and then look for a way to undo it. Eat something before you sit down, alternate a glass of water between drinks, set yourself a limit on the number of glasses from the start and drink slowly. Practice a few polite ways to decline so you keep the relationship without drinking beyond your limit. If your body shows unusual signs that persist, see a doctor.

How do I handle prolonged stress from sales targets?

Sales pressure is a hard-to-avoid feature of the job. The goal is not to remove it entirely but to keep it from taking over all 24 hours. A few short pauses each day, away from your phone and work messages, plus light movement and enough sleep, give the mind room to settle. Breaking a big target into small daily steps also makes the pressure feel more manageable.

I talk all day and get a hoarse, dry throat — what should I pay attention to?

Drinking enough water is the most important thing, because the vocal cords need to stay moist. Sip warm water steadily, avoid speaking loudly or straining your voice when tired, and give your voice a rest between calls. Warm herbal tea also feels soothing for the throat. If hoarseness lasts for several weeks without improving, you should see a specialist.

Driving and sitting in the car a lot gives me a sore back, neck and shoulders — what can I do?

Adjust the seat so your back stays upright, stop to stretch on long drives, and when you arrive, stand up and stretch for a few minutes before your appointment. If you feel numbness in your hands or neck-and-shoulder pain that radiates down your arm and persists, that is a sign to get checked rather than guessing on your own.

Salespeople who sleep little because of early starts and late nights — how can they improve it?

Sleep directly affects your alertness and ability to adapt when meeting clients. If you cannot sleep many hours, prioritize quality: keep a fixed wake-up time, create a wind-down period before bed, and limit late-afternoon coffee and alcohol close to bedtime, since both make sleep fitful.
